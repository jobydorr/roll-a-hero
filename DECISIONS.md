### 2026-08-07 (latest) — Campaign One, revision eight: the Hearthspire opening, the faded go into the towers, and the Mending gets its mechanics

Four clarifications from Joby, arriving on the heels of revision seven and applied the same day. `CAMPAIGN.md` is the state; this records the reasoning, and in particular the answers chosen for the two questions Joby posed openly.

**The faded do not go to the rest villages.** Joby's correction: the villages are the story, and the truth is that the faded go back into the Reactors — into the fold, into holding areas, disappeared, more fodder for suspicion. As applied: the villages are shells (letters answered in a handwriting too even; Hettie visited one and found six cottages, a caretaker, and none of the names — her census upgraded accordingly), the white wagons drive back to the towers, and the faded go down past the sealed doors into holding-creases inside the fold. This merges cleanly with the open question about the Reactor deeps — §8 Q17 becomes *what is done with the faded down there*, with three candidate answers recorded (filed asleep in trust for the Rest; threads drawn off to sweeten the refining; or pure procedure whose reason nobody remembers) — and no-death canon holds: the faded are findable, which turns them from set dressing into rescue material. Joby's "reprocessed/recycled" was deliberately left as the open candidate rather than canon, because whichever answer is chosen needs a satisfying wrong answer in front of it per rule 4.

**The Mending's mechanics, per Joby's constraint that the power originates in healers.** The engine adopted: **a mend is paid for out of the mender** — a thread of their own warmth, the same personal substance the world calls a man's measure; small mends cost a chill and an appetite, great ones visibly fade the healer until rest, warmth, and food restore them, and grandmother's rule is *never give the last thread*. The deliberate resonance is with the faded: a Reactor rips and a mender gives — the same extraction, distinguished only by consent and love — which states the campaign's whole morality mechanically. The concrete guide Joby asked for, without installing a rival deity: **hearth-spirits**, small knots of the world's living tissue, kin to the Compass Spirit, that keep to mender lineages and live in homely things (a stockpot, a button tin — Tobin's rides in his grandmother's darning egg, the correct tool for mending the unraveled). They show and steady but grant nothing, and the D&D translation is direct: hearth-spirit in the deity slot, holy symbol is the homely vessel, spell slots are the threads a mender can safely give in a day, long rest plus a hot meal restores. Joby's "elicit sunsalt usage" became the third payment: a pinch of raw salt spares the mender's thread and can exceed one person's strength, at the price of instability and permanent gray — so every serious mend is a three-price decision (yourself, the world, or the wound). Rival powers to the Devouring were considered and declined again, on the grounds that the mechanics above are *more* concrete than a deity, not less — the cost is visible at the table — and that leaving the rival-power slot empty keeps the Devouring without an opposite number to dilute it.

**The opening moves to the closing of the last Draw and the lighting of the Hearthspire.** Of Joby's two variants, the city-wall version was chosen and the Draw-closing folded into it, because canon had already made the Cinder Draw the last open working — which makes it local, and hands Hettie Sarn her missing scene for free (§8 Q12: the old woman at the rope line who does not clap). The ceremony's three beats each set stage: the honored crews with the faded helped onto white wagons nobody watches leave (planted for the wreck's freight yard, where the ruts end at the sealed door with a crushed garland); the "healing" of the gray, performed by folding it away — the campaign's thesis staged as a miracle, a wound hidden to the sound of cheering; and the lighting, in which the first city-scale pull finds a fold with no slack left and tears. The clock-tower-from-the-sky image survives intact, now caused by the blast. Two structural gains were baked in: the ministry blames the disaster on the tyrant's spite by dusk (feeding the propaganda engine), and the leadership privately learns the jam is nearing its end — which is why the hunt accelerates and why four promising strangers get a clerk so fast. The first dungeon was retuned to match: the wrecked, quarantined Hearthspire (right of search, patched tear, salvage crews), with the living-Reactor infiltration kept as the mid-game template.

**Session one doubles as session zero.** Joby's worst case — no player material before the first session — is now the plan, as a new §6 section with five instruments keyed to the opening's beats: a bounded *why are you at the ceremony* (each option flagging a campaign seam — the faded, the taken and haunted, the Fence War, or competence); the *keepsake* (name what you'd run back for, then threaten it); the *save* (a staged civilian who assumes a history, resolved by bounded choice, with the bullseye kid's civilian staged to their builder picks); *Wenna's questions* (the recruiter closes the session asking, in fiction, what did you lose today, what have you lost before, why did you stand — the session-zero questionnaire administered by the one character whose job is exactly that); and *the thing that does not line up*, seeded once per hero, where any bite is a thread into the haunted. Governing principles: bounded choices for the nine- and ten-year-old, canonize answers by saying them back, never ask twice, and defaults cost nothing.

**Sections rewritten:** §2's Sunsalt history and hiding paragraphs and the Menders section entire; §3's Hettie file; §4's Tobin (darning egg, visible cost); §6's opening (now "The Last Draw and the Hearthspire"), the new "Session One Is Session Zero," "The First Door Is a Machine" (now the wreck), and Act One; §7's rules 17–18 and vocabulary; §8's Q6, Q12, Q17, Q19; §9's Draws, faded, menders, and Mending rows plus new Hearthspire, rest-villages, and hearth-spirits rows.

### 2026-08-07 — Campaign One, revision seven: the Reactors, the Fence War, and the mender in the party

Joby reviewed revision six and sent six adjustments the same day. Four were directives and two were questions, and the answers to the questions were chosen to fall out of machinery the bible already had rather than to add new machinery. `CAMPAIGN.md` is the state; this is the reasoning.

**The Draws become the Reactors, and the damage goes indoors — and then inside.** Sunsalt is no longer scraped from open flats; it is extracted in Reactors, mountain-scale constructs that gnaw power from the deep strata through miles of roots and rip raw sunsalt through tears their condensers open at the crown, with human-crewed processing floors in the middle and sealed deeps no human has described. Joby specified that the environmental damage is hidden and asked how the hiding and the revealing should work. The answer adopted: **the wound lands on the far side of the creases** — every pull bleeds the banished zones gray from within, where no witness who would be believed can go, while the countryside around a tower stays green, which is the entire point of the design. Three supporting mechanisms make the lie complete: the Draws are retained as the *first generation* of workings, closed with public ceremony and blamed on the blight, so the visible gray is a monument to a story rather than an active wound (and it stopped spreading the year the Draws closed, which only Hettie ever noticed); the human damage — long exposure *fades* people, thinning color, appetite, and attachment — is dispersed through crew rotation and pensioned "rest villages" scattered far from home; and the ministry's confession-and-cure theater (mourn the gray, blame the blight, unveil the clean fix) buys it trust with everything else. The reveal is threefold and escalating: Hettie's ledger holds the arithmetic (gray tracked Draw output; Reactor intake never balances; shakes cluster after big pulls), her quiet census of the faded holds the human cost, and the party *walks through* the truth — the first lost place they ever stand in is partly gray, per the house rule that the campaign's crimes are discovered on foot rather than briefed.

**The first dungeon is a Reactor, and it teaches the campaign's grammar.** Joby's directive that infiltrating a Reactor is the first inroad to the folded space slots in cleanly because the condenser tear is the only reliable door: the taken went through sealed gaps, and the tears the towers rip on schedule are the one opening that reliably exists. The dungeon plays in three vertical movements — human floors (stealth and talk, because rule one holds and nobody there is an enemy), Surveyor floors (the party's combat education: wardens who come undone and will be met again), and the crown (be standing in the tear on purpose). The sealed deeps are planted with a satisfying wrong answer (the grinding is "the roots chewing rock") and logged as §8 Q17, with three candidate truths recorded.

**The field still.** The party's late-game raw-sunsalt source is a hand-scale condenser assembled from pieces, per Joby's directive that extraction requires a collected mechanism. The pieces are canon-tied to the folded-away apprentices — one piece per rescue is the obvious pacing shape — which converts a fetch-quest into the campaign's existing rescue engine. Raw sunsalt is unstable because refining is the taming of it and refining happens only in Reactors; the still leaves a palm-sized gray scar at every staking, which preserves the gray-handprint lesson from revisions three through six in a new mechanical body. Instability mechanics are §8 Q18 and should stay one small die, not a subsystem.

**Green Fields gets its war, and Red Flag gets its true origin.** Per Joby, the movement was once openly violent and now works inside a cease-fire within which the Surveyors took true control. The shape adopted: the **Fence War**, a generation of open human fighting against an enemy that could not be made to die, ended at a table by the **Accord** — amnesty, rations, petition, and the right of search, each expedition carrying a Registry clerk (which formalizes Tobin's seat and turns the permitted-lawbreaking loose thread into treaty text). The capture mechanism is the delegation: the negotiators who came back from the peace table were not the people who went, and the veterans who said so — a missing scar, a left-handed man writing right-handed, a laugh a half-second late — were called grief-mad and kept the war's red banner when the movement furled it. That grounds Red Flag's origin in the refusers and the suspicious, exactly as Joby specified, gives the Act One disguise reveal decades of in-world foreshadowing, and accidentally earns the placeholder name an etymology (the flag that did not come down), so §8 Q5 now asks whether Red Flag graduates from placeholder to name. Hettie remains the second, respectable founding — the prover rather than the seed.

**Tobin is a mender, and his friend is inside.** Two directives merged into one rework. Tobin Rell is now a burgeoning cleric — the party's healer through Act One — and the source of clerical power settled the question Joby posed: not rival deities but **the Mending**, the world's own knitting-back, the same living wholeness the Compass Spirit is a fragment of. Every heal is a small unfolding, which is the true and unshared reason healing is outlawed (humans, menders included, are only told mending is "unlicensed"); menders hide as grandmothers and herb-lore, are folded away when caught, and grow stronger as the world unfolds — a second progress barometer beside the steadying ground, and a free stake in the world for any kid who rolls a cleric. This beat rival-gods because it adds no new cosmological actor, gives the healing class a reason to be hunted that connects to the fold itself, and makes the party's mission literally restorative. The discovery *order* is load-bearing and recorded in his file: the party learns his healing secret (a secret they keep *for* him) before they learn his filing secret (the one he kept *from* them), so the betrayal lands on a person rather than a plant. His friend — working name **Ellum Wick** — is no longer alive in a dark house; he went in with an expedition two years ago and is one of the taken, and the party finds his trail on their first venture: clerk's blaze-marks and a cached field book whose pages prove the expeditions are steered and end by naming a town with a dark window. The Act One turn becomes an arithmetic (Ellum's field book beside Tobin's filings) instead of a reunion, Red Flag is inherited through a lost man's handwriting, and Ellum's rescue is a scheduled debt (§8 Q20). No-death canon says he lives; his condition is a tone decision.

**Sections rewritten:** §1's rebellions passage, §2's complaints, Sunsalt (now "Sunsalt and the Reactors"), the Jam, and a new Menders section; §3's Green Fields history and Red Flag origins, plus Hettie's file; §4's Tobin entire; §6's new "The First Door Is a Machine" and the Act One turn; §7's rules 12, new 17–18, the vocabulary table, and the propaganda section; §8 questions 5, 6, 10 revised and 17–20 added; §9 glossary reworked to match.

### 2026-08-07 — Campaign One, revision six: the Surveyors are not human

Joby resolved the open species question himself, and went further than either direction drafted in the `ROADMAP.md` brief. The instruction and its goal come first, because the goal is the design constraint everything else serves: the table includes a nine- and a ten-year-old who act the battles out, and the campaign should carry as little human-versus-human violence as it can. The direction settled: the Surveyors are an openly alien race; the world was conquered and radically altered, but generations of prosperity have made most people accustomed to it and some willing to defend it; the invaders present as benevolent rulers so long as everyone keeps in line; the Foldwright on the throne is false and of the invader species, while the true Foldwright is a human lost in the folds, whose deal with forces from another world — struck to bring humanity limitless power through sunsalt — caused everything; the folding was meant to remake our world into a copy of the invaders' dimension, and he halted it midway, pinning the final evil at the center; the invaders want to complete the process and he wants to unmake it; Green Fields' leadership is the invaders in human disguise; Red Flag remains the only true rebellion; and sunsalt is the dynamic force, killing the human world when harvested and yet the one weapon that can fight the invaders and set the world right. `CAMPAIGN.md` was rewritten end to end; this entry records the reasoning and the connective tissue.

**Two combat rules now deliver the goal directly, and they outrank everything else in §7.** First, *no human being is ever a combat encounter* — humans argue, obstruct, inform, and defect, and the things the party fights are Surveyors, monsters, and eventually the Devouring. Second, *Surveyors come undone rather than die* — struck down, one folds apart into a drift of flat panes and reassembles weeks later somewhere else, remembering. Together these remove corpses and kill-guilt from every fight the campaign contains, and the second buys recurring enemies for free, because the one the party bested in spring can bow to them in fall.

**What the old design cost us, and how each load was re-carried.** The brief priced the swap honestly: the human Surveyors were load-bearing in four places, and a straight species swap would have deleted the campaign's moral architecture. Each load was moved rather than dropped. The intimate betrayal (they deposed their own master) becomes the intimate bargain — the villain's victim is the man who invited him in, and the age's whole grief descends from its hero's signature. The knowing diminishment (they remember losing the craft) becomes the sunk-cost theology: the argument no Surveyor says aloud is that if this world is never finished, the finishing of their own world was not mercy, and everything they are is a crime with a liturgy. The Tuesday-and-Thursday engine survives species-neutral — the same courteous patrol saves the village on Tuesday and folds the protest square shut on Thursday, a hero twice in its own reckoning and an instrument twice in fact. And "unfolding would require them to become men again" becomes "completion is the only outcome that never reopens the question of the first world," which is the same cowardice wearing a longer robe.

**The species is homebrew, and the shape came from the theme.** Beings folded from a single sheet of something not quite paper and not quite porcelain; faces that are crease-patterns, refolded into expressions a half-second late; they fold flat and can leave a locked room under its own door; their name for themselves is not a sound but a gesture of the hands. Their numbers are fixed — every Surveyor abroad came through before the jam, and no more can follow until the fold moves — which single fact explains the soft-handed governing style, the human-staffed Registry, and the patience. Touchstones for the DM's ear only: modron geometry, occupying-civil-service menace, and the courteous wrongness of a Miyazaki masked spirit. The children will name them in a session and the coinage should be adopted.

**The cosmology rework, in one paragraph.** The invaders' home is the Finished World, folded long ago to its last crease; their doctrine holds that an unfolded world is an unfinished one and that finishing is mercy. What actually receives a finished world is the Devouring — their scripture's *Rest*, their sealed ledgers' *Last Fold*. The Folding of our world was a conversion toward that pattern, and the kid-legible image is now an envelope rather than a blanket: a letter is folded in order to be sent. The Foldwright jammed the working at its midpoint — *the jam* replaces *the cinch*, which described a completed fold this canon no longer contains — so the world strains both ways, toward springing open (the tier-two massacre) and toward closing (the delivery). Sunsalt is reworked to match: it is the Finished World's light steeping into ours, refined it folds and raw it unfolds, drawing it kills the land it came from, and it is the only fuel the great Unfold can burn — which keeps Hettie's gray ledger, the gray handprints, and the finale's sacrifice all intact while giving Joby's "dynamic force" both of its faces.

**The two Foldwrights.** The throne is occupied by the First Surveyor — the being that made the bargain — veiled from crown to floor because the face beneath would have to be the face of a man who is somewhere else; the world is taught the working remade its maker into the invaders' kind. This merges the campaign's missing Surveyor-leader slot (old §8 Q7) into the false Foldwright: it is the arguing villain the ending needed, and its Act Three case — everything ends, and the only mercy a world is offered is to end folded and at rest — is the doctrine at full strength. The true Foldwright keeps his hollowing, his measure, his lost family, his zone-scattered losses, and his Act Three temptation, and gains the guilt of authorship, which explains him better than his old savior framing did.

**The trap, named per the standing order.** "The ruling class is secretly another species" is an ugly old allegory template. It is defused here by openness: the Surveyors are *overtly* alien — this is a conquest-and-collaboration story, not a hidden-elites story — and the disguise element is confined to a handful of leadership figures inside Green Fields, where it exists to steer one search. It must not spread into hidden-aliens-anywhere paranoia, and the tell (one crease that does not line up) deliberately rhymes with the portals rather than with any human population.

**Green Fields' new engine.** Its platform softens from "break the tyrant's fist" to "gratitude is not citizenship — human hands on human things," and its beloved arm goes in after *the taken*, people lost through breaches within living memory (a new category, distinct from the haunted, whose losses are unrememberable). Expeditions into the creases are the one coldly-enforced crime, and Green Fields' expeditions are invariably permitted with a Registry clerk attached — which formalizes Tobin's assignment and plants the permit as the campaign's earliest loose thread. Wenna's wound is retuned to fit: they told her that her fourth child was breach-taken and findable, and the truth is the child went into the Folding itself, the one taking her employers will never name.

**What survived untouched, which is most of it.** Red Flag nearly whole — righter than ever, since their program is now also the true Foldwright's, and still catastrophically wrong about the method; Hettie Sarn and the ledger; Tobin's shape and conversion; the opening square scene (the crier's speech retuned to home rule, the two Surveyors at the edge now visibly alien and better for it); the memory theft; the town above; the escalation architecture (rung one is now *bring back the taken*, which lands directly on a kid's chosen motivations); the zone structure; the two map props, now joined by the envelope; and the leveling dependency.

**Sections rewritten:** §1 entire (new history: Bargain, Folding, Halt, Occupation, False Almighty, Standoff), §2's conversion sections and sunsalt, §3 entire, §4's Foldwright and new First Surveyor entry, §5's Devouring, §6's rungs and acts, §7's rules (two new at the top) and vocabulary, §8 (three new questions: a Surveyor defector, the Finished World, the invaders' ending), §9 entire. **New open questions worth flagging to Joby:** what would it take to unconvince the First Surveyor (Q7), does any Surveyor defect (Q14), and what happens to the invaders if the heroes win (Q16).

### 2026-07-14 — Campaign One, revision five: the rebellions change places

Revision four gave the world a third faction. Revision five, later the same day, discovered that the third faction was standing on the wrong side of its own argument — and so was the second. Joby's diagnosis was that the motivations were inverted, and he was right; the fix is a clean swap, and almost everything else fell out of it.

**The swap.** Previously the fake resistance preached *undo the Fold* while the true one preached *hold it forever*. Both are now the other way around, and each is stronger for it.

- **Green Fields (was the Stillguard) does not attack the Fold at all — it attacks the fist around it.** It loves the Fold, because the Fold is why children play at the treeline, and only a lunatic would give that up. What it cannot abide is that the whole of it sits in the hand of one man nobody has ever seen, who seals a road when it suits him, so that a person must beg a tyrant's leave to visit his own sister. Break the fist, keep the Fold, and the world could belong to everyone. It is decent, moderate, popular, and sincere — and it also happens to require somebody to go into the creases and find the man, which is the one thing the Surveyors cannot do for themselves and the entire reason the movement exists.
- **Red Flag (was the Unlit) wants the world unfolded and given back.** Their arithmetic is the same as before and their conclusion is the opposite: the seams exist only because the world is folded, so the burning stops for good on the day the Fold does and not one day sooner. The faction that hates sunsalt most is therefore the faction that wants every door opened, which reconciles the two halves of them that used to sit awkwardly together.

**Why the swap is worth it.** The old fake resistance had to preach the Fold was a catastrophe *while being run by people whose whole project is preserving it*, which never quite held. And the old true faction's program — hold forever, the lost stay lost — was intellectually elegant and emotionally impossible for this table, since its thesis is that the correct answer is to abandon everybody in the creases permanently. The swap fixes both. It also gives the establishment a reason to *tolerate* one rebellion and *hunt* the other, which the old arrangement never explained.

**The cost, and how it was paid.** Inverting Red Flag turns them from the party's sympathetic opponent into the party's ally, which re-opens the exact hole the audit found — nobody left who genuinely knows and genuinely disagrees. Two answers, both of which Joby confirmed and both of which are now in the document. **The sincere half of Green Fields and the public disagree**, on the entirely fair grounds that the Fold works, has worked for a lifetime, and that four strangers with a magic firefly propose to take it away on the strength of something they saw behind a door nobody else can open. **And Red Flag disagrees in the finale**, because when the Devouring turns up they will not flinch — they have been right about everything for their whole existence and it has cost them everyone they love, and *we are not stopping at the last door because there is something behind it* is the same shape as the Foldwright's love for his family, pointed the same way. The people who recruited the heroes are the ones who may have to be talked down.

**Two floors in every faction (Joby's, and it is now the organizing principle of §3).** Each of the three organizations has a leadership that knows what it is actually for and a rank and file that carries out its orders believing something else. The Surveyors are the empire: their leadership is law, their rank and file are law enforcement — men who save a village on Tuesday and break up a rally on Thursday, heroes twice in their own minds and instruments twice in fact. Green Fields' leadership *is* the Surveyor leadership, same room and same purse; its rank and file are sincere reformers whom that same employer's police hunt as anarchists and terrorists. **And they are not wrong to**: a movement agitating to loosen the world's only defense genuinely is dangerous. The consequence is that "what does this faction want" has two answers every time and must never be given one, and that almost everyone the heroes fight is decent while almost everyone who gives an order is not.

**The martyr economy.** A rebellion with no martyrs is not believable, so the leadership lets its own sincere members be arrested and killed by its own police, then grieves them in public and recruits on the strength of the grief. Both halves of that transaction are staffed by honest people, and nobody in the square knows the raid and the rally were ordered by the same hand.

**Both rebellions compete for the haunted, and Green Fields gets there first.** This is the quietest cruelty in the campaign and it resolves what looked like a contradiction. The haunted belong to Red Flag by rights — a woman with a fourth place setting wants what was taken *back*, and only unfolding returns it. Green Fields reaches her first and tells her the tyrant's fist did this to her and that breaking it will make her whole. It is a lie of omission, close enough to the shape of the wound to fit. **So Wenna Ash is recruited against her own interest by the people who caused it**, and is brave for them, and recruits others like herself. Her defection to Red Flag is a late payoff that rhymes with Tobin's early one.

**Recruitment, settled (Joby's fork, and the resolution is worth recording).** *Green Fields recruits; Red Flag inherits.* The recruiter is sincere rank-and-file — a cynical top-level recruiter is both implausible (they would delegate) and a worse character, because the thing that makes Wenna lethal is that everything she says is true and she believes it, so the betrayal does not land *on* her but *through* her. **The leash is not attached by her; it is attached over her head.** She does not question a handler because a movement that has always had handlers does not notice having one, and Tobin reports to people she has never met. The first time the heroes see him file something Wenna was not told about is the first loose thread in the campaign, and it is available in the first three sessions.

**Tobin stays verging, and his conversion is the door.** Red Flag does not recruit the party at all; it inherits them through him. The scene that finishes him is not an argument but being taken to see the friend he has mourned for two years, who is alive in a dark house and could not write — and that single scene proves the rebellion is a puppet, hands the heroes the only real faction in the world, and does both by way of a friendship rather than a briefing. Both of the campaign's gut-punches survive the inversion intact: Act One is *the rebellion is a puppet*, Act Two is *the villain is the victim*.

**Tier one and tier two are now vocabulary (Joby's).** Tier two is the zone monsters, ordinary and fightable and the reason a careless unfolding is a massacre. Tier one is the Devouring, of which there is one, and which is not the king of tier two but a different category of thing. Naming the split plainly is what lets the party spend two acts solving a tier-two problem — *how do we open the doors without loosing all of that at once* — and arrive at the machine, which is a genuinely good answer to the wrong question. **Tier one does not care about the machine.**

**What survived from the discarded austerity faction, and why.** Hettie Sarn, the gray ledger, and the dark window with the tallow candle all transfer to Red Flag without strain, because their content was never the *hold forever* program — it was the counting. She ran the Cinder Draw for thirty-one years, scraped more sunsalt out of the world than anyone alive, and her ledger shows the one fact the whole lie unravels from: **the gray spreads from the workings, not the creases.** The blight has a fence around it, and a payroll, and a schedule. Once a person has seen that the establishment manufactures the disaster it blames on the tyrant, it takes very little to see that it manufactures the opposition too — which is how Red Flag knows what nobody else outside a sealed room knows. *the Unlit* is kept as their older, private word for themselves.

**Red Flag's knowledge boundary, which is the engine of their arc.** They know the crime, the criminals, and the cure. They do not know that opening every door at once hands a world to tier two; they do not know the man they mean to depose was overthrown for trying to do what they are trying to do; and they do not know about the Devouring. **They learn all of it from the heroes, in the field, one door at a time**, which is what turns them from a slogan into a faction over twenty sessions. Their arc is the party's arc and should be run as one: open every door → *we are about to hand a world to those things* → find a way that is not a massacre → the machine.

**Sections rewritten:** §1's invented-tyrant passage, §3 entire (now opening on the two-floor principle), §5 entire (the tier split), §6's opening scene and Act One, §4's Wenna and Tobin, and the §7 rules, table vocabulary, and every-faction-is-partly-right list. **New open questions:** name both rebellions (Green Fields and Red Flag are deliberate placeholders — candidates in §8 Q5); give Hettie Sarn a scene, since she is currently an argument with a ledger and must carry the faction after Tobin leaves; and decide whether Wenna gets out.

### 2026-07-14 (later) — Campaign One, revision four: the clean Folding, the memory theft, and a faction that actually disagrees

The campaign audit's sharpest finding was that our best structural idea had eaten our conflict: with the Surveyors and the Stillguard revealed as one organization, the world contained a single faction, a mood, a prisoner, and a pet, and nothing in it was arguing about anything. This window fixes that, and in fixing it a great deal else fell into place. `CAMPAIGN.md` is the state; this is the reasoning.

**The Folding is believed to have been clean, and the Fold took the memory of what it took (Joby's, and it is the keystone).** Previously the world knew it had lost cities — "every family carries a name it does not say aloud" — which meant the campaign's most horrifying fact was already common knowledge and there was nothing for the party to discover. Now the public believes the Folding cost nothing: the maps are complete, the cities are all there, and the people who vanished were taken by monsters in the bad time before the Fold was drawn tight. That is a half-truth, and it is believable precisely because the other half is true. The mechanism that makes it hold is that **a memory is a relation between a person and a place, and the folding creased those relations shut along with everything else.** The world does not mourn because it cannot. There is no conspiracy of silence about the lost cities; there is no silence and, as far as anyone knows, there are no lost cities.

Three things fall out of this for free, which is how we knew it was right.

- **The Stillguard's recruiting pool changes from the grieving to the haunted.** If nobody remembers, nobody grieves — so the resistance recruits the people the theft failed to take cleanly. The woman who lays four places at a table for three children. The man with a map in his attic of a city no one has heard of. They are called mad their whole lives, they are the only people alive who are right, and the Stillguard offers them the one thing nobody ever has: *you are not mad, there was a city, here is the map.* It is not even a con — every word is true — and the cruelty is entirely in the use it is put to.
- **The Compass Spirit acquires a second job, and it is the party's evidence.** It is already "the thing that remembers how the separated places used to touch," which is memory. So, fed at a seam and set down where a lost thing used to be, **it gives a memory back.** The price of remembering is a gray handprint, every time. It also creates a hard, honest lesson: a memory cannot be handed to anybody, so the proof the party was sent to find is worth nothing to a person who was not standing there.
- **The Surveyors lost the craft.** The knowledge of how to fold went into the creases with the cities, so the order that governs the Fold cannot read it — a priesthood performing a rite whose grammar it has forgotten. This was already implied ("a machine they cannot read"); the memory rule explains it.

**The world visibly folds overhead — the town above.** Joby's Inception reference, and it is far more than texture. Every city has an inverted country hanging in its sky, close enough in the old cities to make out roofs and a clock tower, which its people have looked at all their lives and can never reach. **This makes the propaganda's foundation something the world is *shown* rather than merely told:** the creases everyone can see are neat, shallow, and orderly, which is to say they look exactly like the folded sheet on the pamphlet. Which is why the Snarl, when the party finally stands in it, lands as betrayal rather than as information.

**The third faction: the Unlit.** A group that knows the truth, is not in on the conspiracy, and has a different answer. Grown from Stillguard cells that were sent to look for evidence and looked at the wrong thing — the gray is not spreading from the creases, it is spreading from the workings; the blight has a fence around it and a payroll. Their program is not a slogan: **shut the Draws, put out the lamps, and hold the world exactly as it is, forever.** They are not against warmth; they are against *this* warmth, and a tallow candle in a dark window is their whole doctrine in one image.

Four things make them work where a generic rebel faction would not.

- **Their founder cannot be dismissed.** Hettie Sarn ran a Draw for thirty-one years and scraped more sunsalt out of the world than anyone alive. She keeps the **gray ledger** — the dead land's advance, drawn year by year across ground she used to farm — and her method of recruitment is to open it, turn it around, and wait. She is the world's authority on the damage because she did the damage. She is also the person who most wants to be proven wrong.
- **Their verb is *stop*, and it must stay *stop*.** An early draft had them asking the party to find the machine, which was a fatal collision: that is the Surveyors' want, so the honest faction would have been running a second search party and the whole betrayal would have defused. **They never send anyone to fetch anything.** What they demand of the heroes is the one thing the heroes will not give — *put the creature away*, stop bleeding seams, stop leaving gray handprints across the world to feed a pet. The party will refuse, out loud, repeatedly, and that refusal is the argument the campaign is about.
- **They survive the Act Three reveal**, which almost no opposing faction does. Show them the Devouring and their answer is immediate and defensible: *then let it stay pinned. Sit on the lid. Never open the door.* Which means the lost stay lost — the cities, the Foldwright's family, and whatever each hero lost in there. Their position is a knife aimed precisely at the only thing the party cares about, and it survives full information.
- **They are, without knowing it, the Foldwright's only defenders,** since theirs is the only program in the world that says his work was right. They would be appalled. The line that undoes Hettie Sarn in Act Three is not that the Surveyors lied — she has always known that — but that the man she has called a butcher agrees with her about the lamps, and is the only other person who ever counted.

**The double agent, and why he is a clerk.** Joby's structure: the Surveyors send a handler with the party, and the handler turns. Two corrections were made to it. First, he is **not** a committed Unlit agent at the start — he is a man who has begun to doubt and has been quietly approached and has not yet said yes, so that his conversion is played rather than announced, and so that **the faction does not live or die with him.** Second, the trope of the smooth spy was refused: **Tobin Rell is a junior records clerk**, cheap and expendable and hopeless in a fight, who will spend half the campaign being protected by children. His weapon is that he is a *good clerk* — he writes down what he sees, faithfully, every night, because he has never done a job badly, and a man who records the truth for a year ends up holding a year of the truth. His doubt has a name: a friend who went into the folds with a Stillguard cell and did not come back, whom he has mourned for two years. **The friend is not dead. He walked.** The moment that finishes Tobin is not an argument; it is being taken to see him.

**The mission escalates instead of being briefed (Joby's, and it buys the campaign its length).** The Stillguard's session-one ask is not "find the machine" — a level-3 party cannot attempt that, so it would be a banner rather than a goal — and it is not "find evidence," which is a dossier when a nine-year-old needs a door. It is **"go inside and look."** Nobody alive knows how the Folding was done, and a thing cannot be undone by people who do not understand it. From there the mission climbs six rungs, each bought by walking through a door: *look* → *the portals and the Unfold Rule* → *the Fold cannot hold itself* → *the tyrant has the machine and he is in here* → *he is not a tyrant and your employers want it* → *there is something at the center and the last door is a choice.* **The party's operational verb never changes across twenty sessions** — find the next door, go through, bring back what is inside — while what it *means* changes six times. Two consequences are now load-bearing: **the Armature is not named in Act One** (it is discovered, and its power as a goal depends on that), and **the campaign now has a structural stake in leveling**, because the heroes must credibly become people who could take the machine. Leveling stops being a someday-feature.

**Canon this overturns, recorded rather than buried.** *"Every cell is a search party that does not know it is one"* is gone: they know exactly what they are looking for, and what they do not know is **whose hands it lands in.** This is the better design, because the betrayal is no longer "you were secretly searching" but **"you found it, and you handed it to the wrong people"** — the party is complicit and active rather than duped and passive. Also gone: "every family carries a name it does not say aloud," and the Stillguard recruiting "the grieving and the furious."

**The four-sentence test, which Joby was right to demand.** *A wizard folded the world up small to shut out something terrible, and everyone believes he has been ruling as a tyrant ever since. A rebellion recruits the heroes to go into the folds and find the machine that controls the world. But the rebellion is run by the real villains, who want the machine themselves — and the tyrant turns out to be a broken old man holding the world together in the dark. And the thing he folded the world to stop is still inside it, still eating, and it is getting out.* It passes. The world is intricate; the story the kids play is a straight line with three turns.

**The real complexity risk is vocabulary, not plot.** Eighteen proper nouns is what will glaze a nine-year-old, and the conspiracy is not. `CAMPAIGN.md` §7 now carries **The Words We Use at the Table** — the bible keeps the real words, the table gets plain ones (*the machine*, *the gray*, *the shakes*, *the lost places*, *inside*), and the cinch and his measure are never said aloud at all. Say the real word once, where it sounds best, then let the children settle on their own; their word will be better.

### 2026-07-14 — Campaign One, revision three: the Armature, the manhunt, the house style, and an honest audit

A long campaign-design window. `CAMPAIGN.md` is the state; this records the reasoning so it is not lost.

**The Devouring comes from between, not from an edge.** Joby objected that a world with an "edge" implies a flat disc, and the objection forced a better mechanism. The entity comes through the gaps in things, the spaces inside of spaces, and so it cannot be walled out — only crowded out. The Fold therefore does not bury it behind a barrier; it *leaves it no room*, compressing the world until there is nowhere for the thing to be. This makes **the cinch** the entire point rather than an accident of density. And because nothing can be folded down to nothing, whatever had already entered was swept inward and pinned at the center, where it has been eating outward ever since — **because eating makes room, and room is how it lives.** A breach is not a wall giving way; it is the entity making space for itself. The kid-legible image, which lands instantly: bugs in a blanket, wadded so tight there is nowhere to sit except one lump in the middle.

**Sunsalt (renamed from starsalt) is a motive with a universal adapter.** It does very nearly anything, and it is the fuel by which reality is bent — every wrinkle is paid for in it. Paired with total command of distance (no army marches, no grain arrives, no rebellion gathers without Surveyor leave), the folded world does not make the Surveyors rich, it makes them gods. This repaired the thinnest thing in the design, which was their motive: they are not officials who grew greedy but **gods who were asked to stop being gods and declined.** Their unspoken argument is that unfolding would require them to become men again. Sunsalt is also now **drawn, not mined** — bled up from a seam and crystallized like brine in a salt pan, at workings called **the Draws**, with dead gray land spreading outward from each.

**The Armature, and the standoff it creates (Joby's, and it is load-bearing).** A fold that tight cannot hold itself, so the Foldwright built the instrument that governs it. **His flight into the Snarl was an escape, not an imprisonment, and he took the Armature with him.** That produces a stalemate on four locked conditions: they cannot kill him, because without the Armature the Fold tears; they cannot find him, because nothing charts the Snarl; he cannot come out, because they would take it; and he cannot repair anything, because he is hollow. He has spent a lifetime working the machine in the dark, holding the world together, while the world curses his name for the tremors. **Foldquakes are his grip slipping** — and because his measure is scattered through the cells, the quakes will *ease as the heroes restore him*, so the party can read its own progress in the steadiness of the ground without being told.

**The Stillguard is a manhunt wearing a rebellion's clothes.** Joby was not sold on them, correctly: a dissent-sponge had no teeth. The Surveyors need the Armature and cannot enter the Snarl, so they built an organization that would send other people in — one that recruits the grieving and the furious, points them at the folds, and calls it resistance. **Every cell is a search party that does not know it is one**, and on the day some brave fool finds him, the Surveyors are a step behind. It never wins because winning was never the point. The recruiter believes she is raising an army; she is running a kennel. (Name unsettled — candidates in `CAMPAIGN.md` §8.)

**The craft audit, and what it found.** Three deep passes against `DM_CRAFT.md` (story architecture, journey and dungeon design, players and NPCs). The world scores well: the setting is defined by what it lacks, the limitation deforms the society around it, the Foldwright is a near-complete five-layer character, and the two maps and the gray handprint are textbook physical-evidence delivery. **The game is largely unbuilt, and two findings are structural rather than cosmetic.**

- **There is no seed question.** All three passes found this independently. We have an excellent premise and never asked what the campaign is *arguing about*. Working candidate, from our own material: *is it right to take away something people love in order to save them from it?* A nine-year-old can hold that and genuinely disagree with an adult.
- **The zone structure was a DMPC engine.** Scattering the Foldwright's losses one to a cell means twenty sessions of the table fetching an old man's family and rebuilding his power so that he can matter in the finale. The craft doc's most-corroborated law is that every person at the table gets their thing. **The correction is that each cell must hold a piece of the Foldwright *and* a piece of a player's story** — same prep, doubled payload. This is now the top open question and is flagged at the head of `CAMPAIGN.md`.
- Also found: the Compass Spirit as written is a satnav selling the only route, which makes the journey a rail; the proposed fix is that **the destination is free and the route is paid**, which restores choice and turns sunsalt into the best decision in the game. The zones have no themes or mechanics despite the folding handing us mechanics for free. There is no home base, no starting town, and no clock below the world scale. And the Devouring, being an appetite, cannot carry the philosophical load — that job belongs to the Surveyor leader, who does not yet exist.

**Session zero, without a session zero.** Joby will not run one. The builder already collected traits and motivations for every hero, and that list is unusually well-suited to this world; one kid has already chosen *"to find a lost family member"* and *"to free those who are captured"*, which is a bullseye into the banished zones and should simply be used. The remaining players get **one bounded, multiple-choice question by text** — what did the Folding take from your hero — and whatever they answer goes into a cell. **The recruiter-scene opening was proposed and rejected; do not raise it again.**

**House style adopted.** Joby had §1 rewritten elsewhere after calling my prose "disjointed and somewhat AI sloppish", and supplied his own *Woven Kin* material as the tonal reference. The diagnosis was correct and specific: my register was advertising copy — fragments for punch, one-line paragraphs landing beats, bold three to a paragraph doing work the sentences should do, and italic second-person coaching wedged between facts. **`STYLE.md` now records the rules**, `CLAUDE.md` points at it, and `CAMPAIGN.md` §1 is the reference implementation. §2 through §9 were rewritten into that voice; no canon changed.

**A tooling hazard, recorded because it cost real work.** The Cowork workspace mount **silently truncates large writes**, and it committed a damaged `CAMPAIGN.md` twice and a damaged `DECISIONS.md` once before it was caught. It also cannot delete files, which corrupts git's index and lock files. **Assemble long documents in `/tmp`, `cp` them into the repo, and verify with `wc -c` before committing.** Use `GIT_INDEX_FILE=/tmp/...` when git complains the index is corrupt.

### 2026-07-11 (latest) — A DM craft reference, and the rule for how to read it

**What we did.** Ingested 20 YouTube sources on world-building, campaigns, NPCs, monsters, dungeons, combat, travel and prep, and distilled them into **`DM_CRAFT.md`** (~29k words) — the standing brief for building and running our games. Then built **`DM_CRAFT_QUICK.md`**, a short router card.

**The problem it solves.** A 29k-word reference is useless if loading it costs ~40k tokens every time we want to invent a town. So the two files have strictly different jobs:

- **`DM_CRAFT_QUICK.md` is a *router*, not a summary.** It holds the laws, the build sequences, a **symptom→section triage table** (~45 symptoms → the exact section that fixes each), and the fillable templates. It's ~4k tokens and most prep can be done from it alone.
- **`DM_CRAFT.md` is opened one section at a time**, at whatever the card names. **Never end-to-end.** That's an ~800-token lookup instead of ~40,000.

**Why a router and not a summary.** A summary would throw away the worked examples — which are the actual value — and still be long. A router *increases* the big doc's usefulness instead of competing with it, because it tells you *when* to go deep and *where*.

**Two rules that keep it healthy:**
1. **`DM_CRAFT.md` is the source of truth.** The card contains nothing that isn't in the big doc; if they disagree, the big doc wins. If we ingest more sources, the card needs a sync pass.
2. **⚠️ The craft docs guide the GAME, not the APP.** `DM_CRAFT.md` is a craft reference for world-building and running sessions. It is **not** a source of feature ideas for the DM OS, and must never be mined for `BACKLOG.md` entries. App features get raised by Joby, separately. *(This corrected a first pass that did exactly that.)*

**Where the pointers live.** `CLAUDE.md` (auto-loaded every session), `COWORK_INSTRUCTIONS.md` (the version-controlled copy of the project instructions), and `README.md`.

**⚠️ REVISED, same day — the reference is NOT auto-consulted.** The first version of this made sessions read `DM_CRAFT_QUICK.md` before building any game content. **We reversed that.**

**Why.** A standing order to consult a framework before every creative task is a machine for producing formulaic output. Even with the "net, not a mould" wording, the pull is toward *checking boxes before having an idea* — and the reference is only useful *after* there's something to check. The risk isn't that it gives bad advice; it's that **reaching for it reflexively makes every town, villain, and dungeon come out the same shape.**

**What replaced it.** The standing order in `CLAUDE.md` is now a **brainstorming posture**, not a routing rule: *the first idea is almost always the trope — go past it. Don't recycle. Vary the shape, not the paint. Avoid formula and standardization unless it buys something real for the task at hand. Follow the weird thread. Specific beats generic. Kid-safe ≠ bland.* Plus the tell that we've drifted: **the content is fine, hits all the marks, and is completely forgettable.**

**The craft docs are now pulled, not pushed.** Joby points at them when he wants them. They remain excellent as a **diagnostic** — the triage table maps ~45 symptoms ("combat feels like maths," "players don't care about the world") to the section that addresses each. That's the use that can't homogenise anything, because it only fires when something is *already* wrong.

### 2026-07-11 (later) — Campaign One, revision two: banished zones, and the thing in the middle
Two rounds of stress-testing broke the first draft. The fixes below are canon and **load-bearing**; the reasoning is here so nobody "simplifies" one of them back out.

**Joby's fix: put the danger in the propaganda.** My first draft *removed monsters from the Fold* to protect the Act 3 reveal — a coward's fix that also starved the campaign of things to fight. Joby's version is better and does it properly: the Fold openly walls off the wild places into **banished zones**, and everyone knows unfolding lets the monsters out. So when a player asks *"don't the monsters come back?"* the answer is **yes, obviously, it's on the leaflet** — the question is asked, answered, and closed, and nobody digs further. **The Devouring hides behind a satisfying answer, not behind a blank space.** That's the whole misdirection, and it's sturdier than an omission. It also hands us the encounter engine: **portals lead into the zones**, so the zones *are* the dungeons.

Corollary, now a rule: **every Devouring seed must have a satisfying wrong answer available.** The first draft's seeding method (a word he won't finish, a fear he won't name) was three blank spaces in a row — exactly the pattern a sharp player reads as *there's a second thing in there*. He now speaks **complete sentences the party misreads.**

**Propaganda is now three claims** — unlimited power (starsalt), permanent safety (the zones), and **control** (he can *wrinkle* the world shut: pinch a road, seal a town). The third is the stated crime, and it answers *"why did he do it, and why then?"* completely — he seized the world the moment his machine was finished. A decoy has to answer that or Act 1 leaks.

**The Surveyors were his allies, not usurpers** (Joby). They helped fold the world and knew what it was for. They stopped him from undoing it — *and were not entirely wrong to* — then noticed the starsalt was a fortune and the world easier to hold if its architect were a monster. **The founders knew about the Devouring; the current leadership has inherited the rule without the reason** and reads the sealed warning as their own predecessors' propaganda. That's what keeps the reveal sealed for two acts, and it buys a hell of a beat: in Act 3 the party shows the Surveyor leader the proof and **he goes white**, having laughed at that warning his whole life. And it gives the Foldwright a real flaw — he tried to undo the Fold **for her**, knowing what it might release.

**The two problems that would have killed the campaign at the table, and the one idea that fixed both:**

1. *"Why don't we just stop burning starsalt?"* The chain (burn → world weakens → creases loosen → breaches) made the fix a **lamp ban, not an apocalypse.** A clever kid proposes rationing in session three and is *right*. "The Fold is failing regardless" was asserted, never earned.
2. *Unfolding is indefensible.* The heroes' plan was to deliberately release every monster on earth at once — a thing everyone knows from day one. Two adults and two kids reasonably say **"absolutely not."** "On purpose, prepared" is meaningless while nobody can say what *prepared* looks like.

**The fix — the geometry.** Fold a sheet with something eating in from one edge, and that edge ends up **buried in the middle, wrapped in layers of world.** The Fold never built a wall; it used the world's own body as padding. **The banished zones are the layers. The Devouring is in the innermost crease, still chewing outward.**
- The Fold is failing **because something is eating it.** No lamp ban saves you; burning starsalt only thins the layers faster. (Rationing = "good idea, helps, not enough, and nobody with the power will do it.")
- The **breaches are it** — that's why they worsen, and everyone blames the wrong man.
- The Devouring gets a **location and a clock** a nine-year-old can hold, and the journey gets a **direction**: each portal goes one layer deeper, *toward* it. The last zone is where it lives.

**And it makes the mission defensible — this is now the spine of Acts 2–3.** The wall is coming down either way. *Do nothing* and it snaps at once, into a world with no warning. *Unfold on purpose* and you go through the portals **one at a time**: get the lost people out, learn the land and what lives in it, find the old ways of fighting it, mark the door. Then the world comes undone into a world that **knows what's coming.** Kid version, say it out loud at the table: **"The wall is going to fall. We can be standing there ready — or asleep in bed."** Every region now has a *point* (rescue, scout, prepare) instead of being a checkbox.

**Starsalt physics, corrected.** The old draft said starsalt was destroyed by *burning* but harvested harmlessly — which contradicts itself, and would mean the Surveyors' mines are already enormous public dead zones. **They are, and that's the point.** Starsalt is destroyed by being **taken out of the ground and spent**. **The seams *are* the mines**, everyone can see the grey, and everyone has been told it's *"the Foldwright's blight."* So the Act 1 reveal isn't "there's a dead zone" — it's **"the dead zone is the mine"** — same decoy trick as the monsters. And the party learns it **with their own hands**, cutting raw starsalt for the Compass Spirit and watching the ground die under their fingers. Every direction costs a grey handprint.

**Two smaller fixes:** **wrinkles cost starsalt, a lot** (otherwise the Surveyors would simply box the party in every session), and **portals cannot be destroyed** — they *are* the creases, so the Surveyors can only hide and fence them, which is why they've never removed the problem. **The finale must be a fight with a describable Devouring**, not an abstraction and not a conversation; talking the Foldwright down is a *scene*, not the climax. This table has spent twenty sessions swinging swords.

### 2026-07-11 — Campaign One is *The Folded World* — foundation locked (see `CAMPAIGN.md`)
The first campaign's premise, factions, twists, and act structure are settled after a long brainstorm. The full bible lives in **`CAMPAIGN.md`**; this entry records the *choices* and, more importantly, **why** — because several of them look arbitrary until you see the trap they were dodging.

**The premise: the villain already won.** Joby's instinct, and it's the whole spine — the world opens in the aftermath. A mapmaker, **the Foldwright**, folded the world like a paper map. Heroes must set it right. Travel/journey is the campaign's shape. Tone: Dune + Harry Potter, a splash of Pokémon; no princess-rescuing.

**The design problem we spent the whole session solving — and the fix.** We want a late reveal that *the thing the Fold protects against is the real enemy.* That reveal dies instantly if the public reason to keep the Fold is **safety**, because safety implies a known danger. So the Fold's public benefit **cannot be protection**. It has to be good, and entirely self-contained.

- **The fix: the Fold makes `starsalt`.** The creases wring a glowing substance out of the land — it lights, warms, mends. The folded world is *closer, safer, and richer than it has ever been.* People love it for **prosperity**, not protection. Nothing about monsters. Reveal sealed.
- **And starsalt is the world's life being squeezed out.** Slow (a lifetime, not a season), visible only at the fenced-off **seams**. That's the hidden cost, it's kid-legible, and it means the pleasant world is genuinely damning without looking broken. Joby's constraint drove this: *"a world that is visibly deteriorating is hard to justify as a big bonus."* Correct — so it isn't visibly deteriorating.
- **The Foldwright stays the assumed villain** on the strength of what the Folding *cost*: cities crushed, towns erased, people dead. Propaganda says he did it out of greed, to hoard starsalt. That yields the sentence the whole campaign hangs on: *"A monster did a terrible thing to us. But the world it left behind is nicer, and we've built our lives on it."*

**The two-faction twist (Joby's, and it's the best structural idea in here).** The **Surveyors** (establishment; manage the Fold, hide the portals, broker starsalt) and the **Stillguard** (a loud "resistance" that says the Fold is unsustainable and the Foldwright is a monster) are **the same organization.** The Stillguard exists to absorb dissent — it recruits anyone who senses something's wrong and sends them on missions that go nowhere.

This **retired an earlier, weaker design** where the establishment *suppressed* the truth about the decay. Suppression is fragile; secrets leak. Outsourcing the alarm is sturdier and nastier: the decay is *publicly acknowledged* and therefore permanently parked — *"yes, terrible, the Stillguard are handling it."* Keep it this way. **Rank-and-file Stillguard are sincere** — the rot is only at the top, which buys us sympathetic enemies and future allies.

**The Foldwright's truth, in the order the table learns it:** (1) the world is being eaten; (2) he wasn't greedy — he folded the world to shield it from something, and lost the person he loved in the creases; (3) the thing he was shielding against is real, and unfolding lets it back in. He becomes an **ally**, and the **Devouring** becomes the final enemy. He was *not* driven out publicly — everyone believes he still rules, which is precisely what keeps anyone from noticing the Surveyors actually do. He's been in hiding, searching the folds for her.

**Why he's both help and hindrance (and it isn't a cheat):** he *wants the portals opened* — she's in there — and *dreads the final Unfold* — it's out there. Both, genuinely. That's why he's unreadable for two acts.

**Mechanics locked:** the **Compass Spirit** (a shy companion that unfurls into a projected compass rose) **drinks starsalt** to give the next direction, and its directions are **riddles**. So every leg of the journey is: win starsalt from the dangerous seams → feed the spirit → decipher the marker. Once the party learns what starsalt *is*, every direction they buy costs the world a little — the moral weight is built into the core loop. **The Unfold Rule:** open a portal and travel through it to unlock a region; **no region unfolds until all are unlocked**, then the world unfolds at once. Justification a kid accepts immediately: a folded sheet tears if you pull one crease while the others are set — **demonstrate it at the table with a real paper map.**

**Deliberately unsettled:** the **Devouring** is unnamed and undescribed on purpose — it's the last card, and it must be seeded only in ways that make sense in hindsight. Nobody in the world knows it exists, *including the Surveyors*.

**Then we stress-tested it, and it nearly fell over.** A skeptical read found leaks that would have killed the Act 3 reveal by session two. The fixes are canon now, and the ones below are **load-bearing — don't quietly undo them:**
- **The Fold folded away *distance*, never *monsters*.** The first draft said the wild places "and the monsters in them" were folded away. Combine that with "the world unfolds all at once" and a ten-year-old deduces *"so unfolding brings the monsters back"* — the Act 3 twist, free, in session one. Monsters still exist everywhere in the world; they simply have nothing to do with the Fold.
- **Starsalt is destroyed by *burning* it — the Fold only pressed it to the surface.** The draft said both "the creases squeeze it out" and "every lamp burns a piece of the world," which can't both be true: if the folding does the damage, burning is harmless and giving it back accomplishes nothing. Now: the Fold opened the wound, **burning is the bleeding**, and unburned starsalt is *reabsorbed when the world unfolds*. That is what makes the finale a real choice — *unfold, and everyone gives up the lamps* — instead of a switch.
- **The propaganda blames him for *pride*, not starsalt-greed.** Starsalt didn't exist before the Fold, so "he folded the world to hoard starsalt" is a story that collapses the moment a clever kid checks the dates — and the crack leads straight to Act 2. He folded it to *remake the world and rule it*.
- **Act One's turn is the betrayal + the mechanism, not "the world is dying."** The Stillguard *shout* that the world is dying at every rally, so it can't be a revelation. What's new is what starsalt *is*, and who the Stillguard *are*.
- **He can't do it himself, and now there's a reason.** He cannot walk in the world (most hated face alive), and **the Compass Spirit will not serve him** — it fled its maker and slept a lifetime rather than answer him. It woke for these heroes. He can only follow.
- **The Compass's starsalt cost is local and visible**, not an abstract share of a global total (which is a rounding error next to every lamp in the world, and the kids would say so). They cut it **raw from a seam with their hands, and the ground dies where they cut, in front of them, permanently.**
- **The Foldwright hides in the *creases*** (the unreachable folded interior) — *not* the seams, where the party harvests every leg. Otherwise they trip over him constantly in Act 1, and his dread is a signpost reading "something is out there."
- **Unlock ≠ unfold, and the prop has to show it.** Put a **seal** on every crease of the paper map. Unlocking a region takes its seal off; the map stays folded. The kids watch the checklist shrink and never once ask *"wait, is it unfolded now?"*
- **Act One needs a clock a nine-year-old can feel.** "The world dies in a lifetime" motivates nobody. A **seam is eating a place they love, this season, with people who have names.**
- **The top Surveyors get a real self-justification** (they were cartoon-villainous otherwise, which breaks our own "every faction is partly right" rule). Their best argument is also *true*: unfolding costs real people real warmth *today*, to save a world nobody alive has seen. The heroes have to actually answer that.

### 2026-07-08 — Simplify: an RH main page, and the campaign is a committed file (no server)
Joby's words, after previewing: *"All I wanted was a separate set of pages I can access from the front page… is this really the easiest way?"* It wasn't, and this entry **supersedes** the two below it (the gitignored `campaign/` folder and the loopback-bound local server). The mistake was mine: Joby said the campaign should be *private*, and the only thing they meant was **a player shouldn't be able to reach the DM OS from the character builder.** I heard "hidden even from someone reading the code," and that stricter reading is what forced files-fetched-over-a-server, which forced the launcher, which forced the loopback fix. All of it, gone.

**What we settled instead:**
- **Naming.** The landing page is the **RH main page** (a.k.a. *roll a hero main page*). The **hero builder** and the **DM OS** are the two things you reach from it. Use these names.
- **`index.html` is the RH main page** — a hub with two doors (*Build a Hero*, *Dungeon Master OS*). The DM OS link was **removed from inside the builder**, which is the whole of Joby's privacy requirement: a player in the builder has no path to the DM OS. The passcode on `dm.html` is the gate; that's enough for a family table.
- **The campaign lives in `campaign.js`, committed and public, loaded by a `<script>` tag exactly like `data.js`.** Content we build in Cowork lands there — edit, commit, done — the same way a new spell lands in `data.js`. Joby was explicit: *"I dont care at all about anyone digging through the code."* So there is nothing to hide at the file level, and therefore **no fetch, no server, no launcher, no `file://` problem.** The live site works; double-clicking `dm.html` works. Deleted: `serve.py`, `start-roll-a-hero.cmd`, the `campaign/` folder, the `campaign/` gitignore block; `.claude/launch.json` is back to plain `python -m http.server` (for local dev preview only — nobody needs it to *use* the app).
- The DM's own edits inside the workspace still live in `localStorage` and are layered over the committed base by the existing merge rule, so re-pushing `campaign.js` never clobbers their notes. Verified: a local edit survives a re-sync.

The lesson worth keeping: **when a non-technical requirement ("private") drives a heavy technical design, restate it in plain terms and check it before building.** One clarifying question here would have saved three turns.

### 2026-07-08 — The local server binds to loopback, and there's a launcher *(SUPERSEDED — see above)*
*Kept for the record. The server, launcher, and gitignored `campaign/` folder described here were all removed once the campaign became a committed `campaign.js`. The loopback-binding lesson still stands in principle, but there is no longer a local server that serves private content.*

Two problems, one file. **The launcher:** the DM OS can't be opened by double-clicking `dm.html` — browsers refuse to let a `file://` page read its own folder, so it could never load `campaign/`. That made "start a server" a prerequisite for every session. `start-roll-a-hero.cmd` → `serve.py` now does it in one double-click.

**The hole it closed, which matters more.** `python -m http.server 8000` binds **every network interface** by default. Verified on this machine: while the server ran, `http://192.168.1.213:8000/campaign/docs/` served a full directory listing and every story file to *anything on the home Wi-Fi* — the kids' tablets included. They'd never even load `dm.html`, so the passcode was irrelevant. We had carefully gitignored `campaign/` to keep the story off the internet while leaving it wide open on the living-room network.

`serve.py` binds `127.0.0.1`, and **`.claude/launch.json` runs the same `serve.py`** (with `--no-browser`) rather than its own `python -m http.server`. One implementation, no drift: a fix applied to one launch path and not the other would have been worse than no fix, because it creates confidence without protection. **Do not "helpfully" restore the default bind** to preview from a phone — that re-opens the hole. If you ever genuinely need LAN access, move the campaign out of the served directory first.

Three details that cost real debugging, so they're written down:
- **Find the interpreter, don't assume `python`.** Explorer launches the `.cmd` with the *saved* PATH, which on this machine has the Python Launcher (`py.exe`) but **not** `python.exe`. A shell has both, so a launcher tested only from a shell reports "Python isn't installed" on a machine that plainly has it. The `.cmd` tries `py -3`, `py`, `python`, `python3` and **proves each runs** — `where` also finds the WindowsApps stub that exists, resolves, and does nothing but offer to open the Microsoft Store.
- **`ThreadingHTTPServer`, never `socketserver.TCPServer`.** Single-threaded serving handles one connection at a time; a browser opens several and holds them, so the next request hangs forever and the app appears to freeze. This is why `python -m http.server` itself switched to threading in 3.7.
- `serve.py` sends `Cache-Control: no-store`, retiring the manual hard-refresh ritual for local work. The `?v=` tags in the HTML still matter for the *deployed* site.

### 2026-07-08 — DM tools live on their own page (`dm.html`), and the campaign is never published
This settles the open question that sat in `BACKLOG.md` for a week: *"Where should DM tools live — same app with a 'DM mode' toggle, or a separate companion page?"* **Answer: a separate page in the same repo.** Three reasons:
- **The player app stays kid-simple.** `app.js` is already 1,783 lines; a workspace would double it, and a kid clicking around the character builder could stumble into the DM's spoilers.
- **The shapes are incompatible.** The builder is a single centered column that wipes `#app.innerHTML` on every step. The DM OS is a persistent three-column workspace with drag state, a floating notes window, and live text selection — it needs an inverted render model (delegated listeners, keyed subtree reconciles) that the builder neither needs nor wants.
- **Privacy comes free.** `dm.html` reuses `styles.css`, `data.js`, `icons.js`, and `firebase-sync.js`, but the *campaign content* lives in a **gitignored `campaign/` folder** that never reaches GitHub Pages.

**The passcode on `dm.html` is a curtain, not a vault — and that is on purpose.** Anyone can View Source and read it. It is not the security boundary. **The security boundary is that `campaign/` is gitignored**, so a stranger (or a curious 10-year-old) who gets past the passcode on the live site sees an empty workspace and a `campaign/manifest.json` that 404s. There is nothing there to find. *Do not "fix" this by adding real auth* — you'd break the DM's local workflow and protect nothing that isn't already absent. If the DM ever needs the campaign on a second device, that's the moment to revisit (encrypted files, or Firebase behind a real login), not before.

Two consequences worth writing down:
- **Two data flows, never conflated.** Player data flows **in, read-only** — the OS calls the existing `RAHSync.listCampaign()` to see the party's shared heroes. Player HP the DM tracks during a fight lives only in the DM's tracker and **never writes back** to a player's hero. Story data flows **nowhere**.
- **`dm.html` borrows the hero math rather than copying it.** It loads `app.js` for a new `window.RAH` export block (and `app.js`'s boot is now guarded by `if (document.getElementById('app'))`, since `dm.html` has no `#app`). Copying `computeHP`/`computeAC` would mean the initiative tracker eventually shows a different AC than the player's printed sheet — at the table, in front of a 9-year-old. ⚠ `withState()` is **sync-only** (`try/finally`): hand it an `async` function and it restores `state` at the first `await`, then silently computes the wrong hero.

### 2026-07-08 — A requirements engine drives completeness, editing, and (later) leveling
Rather than bolt on an "edit character" screen, we added **one declarative `requirements(snap)`** that returns every choice a hero still owes, each tagged with the level it unlocks at. Everything reads from it: the ⚠ badge on the saved-hero list, the Edit screen's "Fix this →" jumps, and `classComplete()`. **When leveling arrives, new requirements simply appear at higher levels and the same screen points the player at them** — leveling becomes "satisfy new requirements," not a separate system. Two enabling model changes: a per-character `level` (replacing the hardcoded global in HP math) and `archetypeChoice` (mirroring the proven `race.choice`). A `normalize()` pass backfills both on load, so heroes saved by the old app open without error.

### 2026-07-08 — Animal companions: PHB rules, hand-curated stats, three tiers
The Beast Master roster is **every Beast the rules allow** (Challenge 1/4 or lower, Medium or smaller) — 42 animals. Two calls worth remembering:
- **The Monster Manual scrape is not trustworthy.** Its stat blocks are two-column and the OCR bleeds adjacent creatures together — it "gave" the frog a +9 attack and the hawk a piranha's Blood Frenzy. Name / size / speed / AC sit on fixed lines and extracted cleanly; **attacks and tricks were hand-curated.** If anyone re-derives this data, don't trust an automated scrape.
- **Three tiers, not 42 stat lines.** `fierce` (+6, 1d6+4), `swift` (+6, 1d4+3), `gentle` (no attack — the card says so plainly, so a kid learns it before a fight, not during one). HP is `4 × level` **straight from the PHB's Ranger's Companion**, so it scales itself when leveling lands; the proficiency bonus is pre-baked into AC and attacks so nobody does arithmetic at the table.
- 42 choices would overwhelm a beginner, so the picker borrows the trick the app already uses for classes: **narrow first, browse second** — a "what do you want your friend to do?" filter, then the PHB's classic examples, then groups, plus search.

### 2026-07-07 — Character sharing MVP built on Firebase (compat SDK, anonymous auth, Firestore)
Implemented the sharing feature end-to-end and verified it against the live project. Technical shape, chosen to fit the app's no-build, `<script>`-tag style:
- **Firebase compat SDK** loaded from the gstatic CDN as plain `<script>` globals (not the ES-module build) — no bundler needed. Config is a global in `firebase-config.js`; `firebase-sync.js` exposes `window.RAHSync`. If the SDK/config can't load, the app **degrades gracefully to offline-only** (sharing UI hides; build/print/backup still work).
- **Anonymous Auth** gives every browser a stable id with no passwords/emails (kid-safe). **Firestore** stores shared heroes at `campaigns/{code}/characters/{ownerUid__charId}`, with the full local snapshot under `character` so the DM prints an identical sheet.
- **Local-first preserved:** `localStorage` stays the source of truth; Share only *publishes a copy*. New **Export/Import** (single hero or full backup) is the safety net and never deletes.
- **Security:** `firestore.rules` (in repo) — anyone signed in can *read* a campaign (so the DM sees the party; the campaign code is the practical gate), but you can only *write/delete your own* heroes. Still needs pasting into the console to replace "test mode."

### 2026-07-07 — Big question SETTLED: add a small backend (local-first) for character sharing
The open architectural fork is **decided: we're adding a small backend.** Joby wants easy sharing — one site where users make a profile, build characters, hit **Share**, and the selected characters appear on a **common page the DM can see**, ending the "friends have to send me a PDF" problem. **Guardrails locked in:**
- **Local-first — nothing is ever lost.** Each browser's `localStorage` stays the source of truth; the backend only *publishes a copy*. If the backend ever vanished, every hero still lives in its owner's browser and in exported files.
- **Existing characters are protected (the non-negotiable).** The **first** build step, before anything else, is **Export/Backup** to capture the two characters friends have already made. Migration is copy-out-then-up — never move, never delete.
- **Kid-safe.** No passwords/emails for kids — profiles are display names + a shared **campaign code**, invite-only, zero personal info collected.

**Phased plan:** (0) Export/Backup → (1) profiles + Share-to-campaign → (2) live DM party page.

### 2026-07-15 — Reference library widened to classic modules across editions
Added six older adventures and supplements to the repo's reference shelf: B2 *Keep on the Borderlands* (Basic), T1 *The Village of Hommlet*, N1 *Against the Cult of the Reptile God*, *Book of Challenges: Dungeon Rooms, Puzzles and Traps* (3e), *Dungeon Delve* (4e), and H1 *Keep on the Shadowfell* (4e). The reasoning is that the classic modules are the best available teachers of *structure* — how a village is populated, how a dungeon is laid out and paced, how a puzzle or trap is fair rather than cruel, how a faction's territory reads on a map. That craft is edition-agnostic and it is exactly what we want to learn from, whereas the mechanics of 3e and 4e are not merely unnecessary but actively incompatible with our simplified system. The same rule therefore governs this material as governs the 5E books, only more emphatically: mine them for bones and texture, never for stat blocks or math.
