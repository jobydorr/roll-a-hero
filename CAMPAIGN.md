# Campaign One — *The Folded World*

*The campaign bible. What is true, what the world is told, and what the heroes find out — in order.*

*Foundation laid 2026-07-11; revised twice on 2026-07-14 to invert the two rebellions, and revised again on 2026-08-07 — revision six, which this document now reflects. Revision six changes the nature of the Surveyors, splits the Foldwright in two, and rewrites the purpose of the Folding itself; the reasoning is recorded in `DECISIONS.md`. Prose follows `STYLE.md`; §1 is the reference implementation. Before changing anything, read §7, The Load-Bearing Rules — several of them exist to keep the later revelations from leaking, and breaking one will quietly break the campaign.*

*One warning belongs at the front. **The players are not in this document.** There are four heroes with no stakes, no hooks, and no wounds, and until that is corrected we have a world rather than a campaign. It is the first open question in §8 and it outranks everything else there.*

## 1. The Story

### The Bargain

The world was wide once, and slow, and human. Distance was the tax on everything: armies moved or they did not, grain reached the city or it rotted, a message arrived in time or a year too late, and in the wild places between the settled ones lived monsters that ate whole villages, in the years people still call the bad time. It was not a dying world, but it was a world that paid dearly for every good thing it had.

The most gifted wizard of his age believed the tax could be repealed. He was a man capable of reaching past the surface of the world and taking hold of the fabric beneath it, and what he wanted was not a throne but plenty — an end to want, an end to distance, an end to the bad time, delivered to the whole of humankind at once. Reaching deeper than anyone had reached before, he found marks in the fabric that his world had never made, creases left by hands that were not hands, and something on the far side of them noticed him noticing.

What answered was courteous, patient, and precise. The beings who came through to meet him did nothing for the first year but measure — the roads, the rivers, the distances between one grief and another — and so the name the world eventually gave them was the only one it ever needed: the Surveyors. They showed him a crystal of their own world's light, which we now call sunsalt, and there is nothing it cannot do. And they showed him the arithmetic that came with it: a folded world bleeds sunsalt the way a pressed orchard bleeds cider. Fold your world, they told him, and want ends. The price they named was small, and the price they did not name was everything.

He accepted. It is the oldest story there is — a man of real gifts and real love reaching for a power he has not understood, from partners he has not questioned, because the answer was too beautiful to interrogate — and he is called the Foldwright because of what he did next.

### The Folding

The working was his and the design was theirs. Under their instruction he built the Armature — a geometric construct of nested rings and sliding cells, like a labyrinth that can be turned in the hands — which is both a map of the Fold and the mechanism that governs it, and through it he began to fold the world. The wild lands were folded away into sealed pockets, and the monsters of the bad time went into them, which is the half of the promise that was kept. The far places were folded close together. Distance began, everywhere at once, to end.

The price came out of the creases. Entire cities went in, along with towns, roads, harbors, and every person living in them, and because a memory is nothing more than a thread between a person and a place, and the folding creased those threads shut along with everything else, the world did not merely lose those cities. It lost the knowledge that it had ever had them. The names went. The roads that led there went. The songs went, or lost the verse that mattered. The grief went, mostly. How much the world consented to any of this is a question the world can no longer answer, because the Folding took the memory of the asking.

### The Halt

Midway through the working, the Foldwright read the pattern he was pressing into the world, and understood it.

The fold was not converging on a shelter. Crease by crease, the world was being made into a copy of the Surveyors' own dimension — folded, finished, closed — and the purpose of that shape is not safety. A letter is folded in order to be sent. The world was being made into an envelope, and at the center of the half-made fold, where the creases converged, the recipient had already begun to arrive: a thing the Surveyors' own scripture calls the Rest, and their ledgers call the Last Fold, and this document calls the Devouring, because that is what it does. His own wife and children were in the creases by then, folded away with everyone else's, and he alone in all the world could still remember their faces.

So he did the one thing left to him. He stopped. He wrenched the Armature against the whole weight of the design and jammed the working at its midpoint, and the world has been a half-folded thing ever since — no longer what it was, never to be what it was being made into. The stroke caught the Devouring in the closing of it: everything of the entity that had already come through was pinned at the center, half arrived, locked out of its delivery. And the stroke cost the Foldwright his measure, the greater part of his own substance, spent in one motion against a design built by gods. He came out of the halt hollowed — a fraction of the man who went in.

The Surveyors came for the machine, and for him. He fled into the one place they could not follow — the Snarl, the deep interior of the creases, where nothing can navigate — and he took the Armature with him.

### The Occupation

The Surveyors did not take revenge, because revenge is disorder. They ruled.

They had come through in numbers before the jam, and no more of them can follow until the folding moves again, so there have always been fewer of them than the world assumes, and they govern accordingly — with soft hands, human clerks, and tremendous patience. The roads are kept. The rations are fair. When a crease gives and something with teeth walks into a wheat field, the Surveyors arrive within the day, put the monster down in public, and fold the gap shut behind it. A person who keeps in line will live a comfortable life and be treated with unfailing courtesy, and most people keep in line, because the folded world is warm, safe, and prosperous, and because nobody alive remembers any other one.

They cannot be killed here, and the world knows it. A Surveyor struck down does not bleed and does not die; it comes undone, folding apart into a drift of flat panes that slide away along creases in the air, and some weeks later it is somewhere else, itself again. The world has noticed, and has drawn the intended conclusion, which is that fighting them is pointless rather than wrong.

### The False Almighty

One problem could not be governed away, which is that the world knew the Folding had been the Foldwright's work. His name was on the age itself. Rather than erase the name, the Surveyors claimed it.

The world is therefore taught that the working remade its maker — that the Foldwright passed through his own fold and came out the far side of being human, finished, the first and only man ever raised into the Surveyors' kind — and that he rules. What sits the high seat is a figure a head taller than any man, veiled from crown to floor, because none may look upon the face that folded the world. Every decree is issued in his name. Every ration, every fence, every sealed road is his will, regretfully administered by his ministry, who are ever so sorry about the fences.

The truth of the veil is administrative rather than mystical. The face beneath it would have to be the face of a man who is somewhere else — and the thing wearing the title is the First Surveyor, the same being that made the bargain a lifetime ago, keeping a chair warm for a name it stole. The arrangement earns its keep three times over: the name carries absolute authority, the veiled figure absorbs every cruelty so that the courteous ministry keeps its clean hands, and the world's anger is given a single distant target that can never be reached, questioned, or petitioned.

### The Standoff

The flight into the Snarl created a stalemate that has now held for generations, and it rests on four locked conditions. The Surveyors cannot finish the fold, because the great design is jammed by its own instrument and answers to nothing else; every wrinkle they pinch is a surface crease, and the deep working will not move without the Armature. They cannot find the man who holds it, because nothing can chart the Snarl. He cannot come out, because the moment he did they would take the machine at the door. And he cannot unfold the world himself, because the halt hollowed him, and the unmaking of a god's design needs more man than he has left.

So he sits in the dark and holds the jam with the last of his strength. A half-folded thing strains in both directions — toward springing open, and toward folding shut — and he holds it at the crease, day upon day, for a lifetime. The foldquakes that crack walls and drop bridges are his grip slipping, and they come more often now. The Surveyors tell the world the shaking is the settling — the world easing into its finished shape — and the world believes them, and shivers, and blames the veil.

### The Two Rebellions

Against all of this stand two rebellions, and only one of them is real.

Green Fields is the loyal opposition the world loves: grateful for the Fold, warm toward its keepers, and asking only that human hands hold human things. It is decent, moderate, popular, sincere from the waist down — and steered from the top by Surveyors folded into human shapes, because the one thing the invaders cannot do for themselves is send searchers into the creases after the man with the machine. Red Flag is small, dark-windowed, and right: it knows the gray is manufactured, knows the rebellion is on a leash, and wants the world unfolded and given back. Both are discussed in §3, and the heroes will pass through one on their way to the other.

### The World as It Stands

And so the world has its arrangement: an alien order governing kindly in a stolen name, a veiled figure on a high seat absorbing the world's anger, a rebellion hunting a fugitive on its owners' behalf — and, in the dark at the center of everything, a hollowed man holding a half-folded world at the crease with both hands, while the thing that was meant to receive it eats, and eats, and makes room.

## 2. The World

### The Folded World

The world is close, it is safe, and it is rich.

A grandmother lives a day's walk away rather than a season's journey, and the roads between are short and empty of threat. The folding tamed the wild, and the truly dark things — the old horrors that once ate whole villages — were folded outright into pockets from which they cannot reach anyone. Children play at the treeline. Doors go unbarred at night.

The world is also prospering as it never has. Sunsalt lights and heats and mends and drives, it does what nothing else has ever done, and every year some workshop finds a new trade to put it to. No generation has lived this well.

It is also, openly and unremarkably, an occupied world. The Surveyors are not hidden and have never pretended to be human; they are simply *there*, the way weather is there — tall, courteous, few, and permanent — and a person born under them finds them no stranger than the sky. Most people, asked, would say the world is better than it was, and they are neither fools nor wrong, and a fair number would defend the arrangement with some heat, because the arrangement is the only thing standing between their children and the monsters everyone knows are still in the creases. Nor does anyone believe they paid anything for it, because as far as the world remembers, the Folding was clean.

What complaints exist are complaints about the present rather than the past, and there are three of them. The ground shakes: foldquakes crack walls, stop clocks, and drop bridges into rivers, and they come more often now than they did, though the ministry calls it the settling and insists it is nothing to fear. Where sunsalt is drawn out of the world the land goes gray and does not recover, and the fenced gray is a little wider every year. And any road a person walks can be sealed at a word, in the name of a veiled figure nobody has ever petitioned and nobody ever will.

All three grievances are real, all three are permitted, and all three point at the Foldwright rather than at the courteous beings who administer them. That is not an accident.

This is the trade the campaign is about: a close, safe, prosperous, shuddering world that has been robbed and does not know it, ruled by its robbers and largely content, and something badly wrong underneath the floor.

### The Visible Fold

The folding is not hidden. It is the sky.

Look up from any street in the world and, past a certain height, the land comes back — hazy, inverted, immense, the underside of some other country hanging where the clouds ought to thin out. Light comes through it the way light comes through weather, and nobody has ever thought this strange, because nobody alive has seen a sky without it. Children are taught the shapes overhead the way children elsewhere are taught constellations.

In the older cities the fold above hangs close, and one can make out roofs, and roads, and the hands of a clock. Every city of any size therefore has a town above: a place its people have looked at their entire lives, whose silhouette they know by heart, and which they can never reach. Where the two hang closest they can very nearly see one another, and it has occurred to nobody to wave.

This does a great deal of work for the propaganda, and it should be understood as the propaganda's foundation rather than as decoration. The creases everyone can see are neat, orderly, and shallow — which is to say they look exactly like the tidy folded sheet the ministry prints on every pamphlet. The diagram is therefore not merely a lie the world has been told; it is a lie the world has been shown, every day, since birth. And that is why the Snarl, when the party finally stands inside it, does not land as new information. It lands as betrayal.

### What the Folding Took

Three things went into the creases, and only the first is obvious.

It took places. Cities, towns, roads, harbors, whole provinces, and the people who were living in them at the time, none of whom were warned and none of whom came back.

It took memory, and the image to use for this is a thread. A memory is a thread running from a person to a place, which is to say it is a kind of distance, and the folding creased the world's distances shut without troubling to ask what was tied to them. The threads went into the creases with everything else. This is why the world does not mourn: there is no conspiracy of silence about the lost cities, because there is no silence and there are no lost cities, only a complete map, an unbroken history, and a population that would be baffled to be told otherwise. And it is exactly what the haunted are. They are the people left holding one end of a thread that goes into the fold and does not come back, and who cannot say what is on the other end of it, and cannot put it down.

And it took ideas, which is the strangest of the three and the one to spend most carefully. Some things the world knew are simply not known anymore, and nobody can miss them, because a person cannot notice the absence of a thought they have never had. The clearest case is the old craft of survival: the wild went into the pockets, and the knowledge of how to fight what lives there went in with it, so the world that will one day have to stand in front of an opened door has forgotten how to hold a spear-line, how to ward a village, how to read the weather of monsters. That knowledge is not destroyed. It is inside, with the people who had it, which is one more reason the doors matter.

Against all of this stands one exception, and the campaign runs on it. The Compass Spirit is the world's memory of how the places used to touch, which means it is the only thing left on this side that remembers — and it can give a memory back. That is discussed in §4, and it is the party's evidence.

### What Everyone Believes the Fold Is

The ministry prints the same diagram on every pamphlet. The world is a sheet of paper, folded neatly over on itself and put away safe — the wild shut into its pockets, the bad time closed like a finished book, everything precious tucked inside the crease where nothing can reach it.

The diagram is teachable, memorable, corroborated by the sky, and, for its intended audience, entirely sufficient. No human alive knows otherwise: not the public, not either rebellion, not the clerks who stamp the ministry's forms. The Surveyors, by contrast, know precisely what the Fold is, because it is their own craft and their own home's pattern — and that asymmetry should be felt at the table. Humanity is living inside a working it cannot read, administered by the only beings who can.

### What the Fold Actually Is

The Fold is a conversion, halted halfway.

Crease by crease, the working was remaking this world into a copy of the Surveyors' dimension — the place their doctrine calls the Finished World — and a finished world is not a shelter. A letter is folded in order to be sent; the world was being folded into an envelope, and the envelope had a recipient. For a child at the table, that is the entire cosmology in one image, and it should be delivered with a real envelope in hand: *this is what they were making. A folded letter. And a letter gets delivered to someone.*

The Foldwright jammed the working at its midpoint, which is the world's whole situation in one fact. The fold is half-made, and a half-folded thing is not stable: it strains toward springing open, which would loose every pocket at once onto an unready world, and it strains toward closing, which would complete the delivery. The recipient itself — everything of the Devouring that had already come through to receive the world — was caught in the halt and pinned at the center of the fold, half arrived, and it has been eating outward ever since. It eats because eating makes room, and room is the only thing it needs in order to finish arriving.

### The Snarl

The interior of the Fold is not a stack of neat layers. Crumple the paper and then force the wad into a box until it cinches tight, and the result is closer to the truth: a dense, convoluted, doubled and redoubled labyrinth packed into a shape, with the texture of a brain squeezed into a geometry. There are chambers within chambers, passages that fold back through themselves, and rooms larger inside than the space that appears to contain them.

The banished zones are the cells of this structure, and each cell is itself densely folded, which gives them their character: a forest that loops back on itself, a town in which the same street occurs three times, a mountain whose interior is larger than its exterior. Depth still means proximity to the center, but the route inward is not a line and cannot be reasoned out from the outside.

Two things in the world can navigate the Snarl, and no third. The first is the Armature, which is a map of the Fold as well as its mechanism, and which tells the man who holds it where the cells lie without telling him what is inside any of them — so he can walk the labyrinth for a lifetime and never find a door he is able to open from within. The second is the Compass Spirit, which is the world's connective tissue and the only thing that still remembers how the separated places used to touch. Everyone else who has gone in has simply wandered until they stopped. That fact does quiet work for Green Fields, whose expeditions were cast into the creases as a dragnet and would have wandered regardless.

### The Jam

The world is folded to its midpoint and held there, and the holding is the whole of current history. The Foldwright called the halted position the jam, and it replaces any notion of the fold being complete: completion is precisely what he prevented, and precisely what his enemies intend.

Four consequences follow, and each of them matters.

Nobody can fold their way out of this, and nobody can fold their way forward either, except through the Armature. The deep design answers only to its instrument; the wrinkles the Surveyors pinch are surface creases, made by hand at ruinous cost, and they no more move the great fold than pleating a sleeve moves the arm inside it. This forecloses the obvious question — why do the invaders not simply finish the job by hand — with a hard mechanical answer rather than an evasion.

The Devouring eats, and eating produces slack, and slack is space. A breach is therefore not a wall giving way. It is the thing at the center making room for itself, and something with teeth wandering out through the gap it left.

The Fold holds at the jam only because one hollowed old man is still working the Armature. Foldquakes are his grip slipping, and they will worsen as he weakens. They will also ease as the heroes restore what he lost, which means the party can read its own progress in the steadiness of the ground without anyone ever explaining the connection.

And the cruelty at the heart of the arrangement is that the lamps and the thing at the center are doing the same work from opposite ends. Sunsalt is the Finished World's own substance, and burning it seasons this world a little further toward that one — every warm evening is a small vote for completion, cast by people who have no idea an election is running.

### Sunsalt

Sunsalt is rationed by the ministry in the Foldwright's name and sold as limitless. It is neither limitless nor a gift, and it is not, in the oldest sense, from here.

It does very nearly anything. It lights and heats and mends, it drives what should not move and holds up what should have fallen, and it is the fuel by which reality itself is bent — every wrinkle the Surveyors pinch is paid for in sunsalt, and so is every human shape their leadership wears. This is not lamp oil, and understanding that is necessary to understand why the invaders will never willingly let go of it.

What sunsalt actually is, is the light of the Finished World, steeping into ours. The folding is a conversion, and where it has bitten deepest the world's own substance is being replaced by theirs; at those places — the seams — the foreign element crystallizes and can be drawn. Taking it is not harvesting but amputation. The patch of world it is drawn from goes gray and dead and stays that way, because what is being scraped up is the replacement for something that no longer exists.

It is not dug but drawn. From a seam the crystal can be bled up and left to cure, in the manner of brine in a salt pan. The workings are called the Draws: broad glowing flats, fenced and guarded and visible from a mile away, with crews scraping the crust. Around every worked Draw the land is gray and dead, and the gray is spreading. Everyone can see this, and everyone has been told that it is the Foldwright's blight, seeping up out of the creases.

One asymmetry in the substance is the campaign's arsenal, and it should be discovered rather than announced. Refined sunsalt folds: it is the fuel of every wrinkle, every disguise, everything the invaders do. Raw sunsalt, bled straight from a seam, unfolds: touched to a crease it loosens it, touched to a folded thing it urges it open, and it is the only fuel the great Unfold can ever burn. The same substance that is killing the human world is the one weapon the human world has, which is why Red Flag's arithmetic — a handful of gray handprints now, to end the Draws forever — is an argument they have already had with themselves and won. It is also exactly the kind of argument that turns out to cost more than the arithmetic showed.

The party will learn all of this with their own hands. Refined ministry sunsalt is beyond their reach, so they bleed it raw from a seam with their fingers, and the ground dies beneath their hands and stays dead. They leave a gray handprint behind every time they want a direction from the Compass Spirit, and another every time they want a memory back. Nobody will need to explain sunsalt to them, because they will have done it themselves.

### Breaches

A crease gives, and something with teeth walks into a wheat field. Breaches are worsening every year, and everyone has an explanation for them.

The ministry says the breaches are the settling's growing pains, that the Foldwright in his wisdom permits what he permits, and that with more fences, more wrinkles, and more patience the world will come to its rest. Then the Surveyors arrive, unfold to their full height in the village square, put the monster down with terrible efficiency, seal the gap, bow, and leave — which is precisely how they earn and keep their welcome. It allows them to appear as the world's reluctant shield, and it is a very good racket.

Green Fields says the breaches prove the point: a ministry that can seal a road at breakfast could seal a crease if human voices sat at the table where sealing is decided, and the world should stop being grateful and start being represented.

The truth is that the entity is making room for itself, and that every lamp in the world is helping it.

Because breaches are visible, local, urgent, and fightable, they do four jobs at once: they supply the campaign's early pressure, they supply its monsters, they supply the taken — the people snatched or lost through a gap before it closes, whose absence, unlike the Folding's thefts, is remembered and mourned — and they supply its central irony, since the people thanking the Surveyors for their protection are being protected from the consequences of the Surveyors' own project.

### The Banished Zones

The banished zones are sealed pockets holding the world's wild places. Each portal opens into one, and each sits deeper than the last. Some contain monsters. Some contain lost towns and lost people, swallowed in the Folding and still inside. Some contain the folded-away — because the ministry does not execute anyone, ever; people who will not keep in line are *put away*, folded into holding-creases with courteous regret, and the world considers this proof of the government's gentleness rather than asking where the put-away go. And some contain what the Foldwright lost, which is discussed in §4.

### Wrinkles

The Fold is a living thing, and a fresh crease can be pinched into it anywhere — sealing a road, quarantining a village, stranding a party on the wrong side of a valley. This is the visible basis of Surveyor power. No army marches, no cargo arrives, and no rebellion gathers without their leave.

Pinching a wrinkle does not require the Armature, and the distinction matters. A wrinkle is a crude local crease made by hand at ruinous cost in sunsalt, and the Surveyors can make one anywhere they please. The Armature is the instrument that governs the deep design, and they do not have it. So they can fold a road shut and they cannot move the great fold one crease in either direction, which is the exact condition of a locksmith locked out of his own shop, able to jam every door in the street and unable to touch the one that matters.

Because wrinkles are made in the Foldwright's name, a road slamming shut in front of the heroes will read to them as the almighty hunting them personally. He is not. The man whose name is on the wrinkle is helping them, and the misreading is available to run for two full acts.

Wrinkles cost a great deal of sunsalt, which is what limits their use. Large ones are events rather than routine, and a party that learns the price can bait the ministry into overspending.

### Portals and the Unfold Rule

Every folded region has a hidden portal, and a portal is not a door. It is a place where the crease shows, which is to say a place where two things that should line up do not: a garden wall whose stones do not match across a single course, a stream that runs into a hillside and never comes out the other side, a stair with one step too many that nobody in the house has ever counted. The world is full of such places and everybody has walked past one, because a thing that does not line up is not frightening, it is merely shoddy. The Compass Spirit's riddles are riddles precisely because this is what it has to describe.

To unlock a region the party must open its portal and travel through it, and no region can unfold until every region has been unlocked, at which point the world unfolds all at once. The reason is mechanical: a folded sheet tears if one crease is pulled open while the others remain set.

Portals cannot be destroyed, because they are the creases themselves. The Surveyors can hide them, fence them, and wrinkle the roads around them, but they cannot unmake them, which is why the problem has never simply been removed.

## 3. The Factions

### How All Three Are Built

Three organizations matter in this world, and all three are built the same way, which is the most useful single fact to hold while running them. Each has a leadership that knows what the organization is actually for, and a rank and file that carries out its orders while believing something else entirely. The gap between those two layers is not incidental decoration. It is where every betrayal in this campaign lives, and every sympathetic enemy, and every ally the party will ever recruit.

The practical consequence is that "what does this faction want" is a question with two answers every time, and it should never be given one. It also means that almost everyone the heroes meet is decent, and almost everyone who gives an order is not — and one further rule now sits on top of both, recorded in §7 and repeated here because it shapes every encounter: the people the heroes *fight* are never people. Humans in this campaign argue, obstruct, inform, and defect. The things that stand in the party's way with force are Surveyors, monsters, and eventually the Devouring, and not one of them leaves a body.

### The Surveyors

The Surveyors are the empire, and they have never needed to conquer anything, because they were invited.

To look at, a Surveyor is a tall, narrow person folded out of a single sheet of something that is not quite paper and not quite porcelain. Its face is a pattern of creases, and it makes expressions by refolding them, deliberately and a half-second late, in the way of a traveler consulting a phrasebook. It moves with perfect economy and speaks with unfailing courtesy, and everything about it is very slightly wrong against the light. It can fold itself flat, which means a Surveyor can leave a locked room under its own door, and it fights — when it must, which is rarely — by unfolding: extra limbs, sudden height, edges where a body should have surfaces. Struck down, it does not bleed and does not die. It comes undone, parting along its creases into a drift of flat panes that slide away through the air and are gone, and some weeks later it is somewhere else, itself again, and it remembers. The children at the table will name these beings inside of a session, and whatever the children pick will be better than anything in this document. Their own name for themselves is not a sound at all but a fold — a precise gesture of the hands — and humans in their service learn to make it clumsily, the way one salutes in a borrowed army. For the DM's private ear, the blend to reach for is the modron's geometry, the quiet institutional menace of an occupying civil service, and the courteous wrongness of a Miyazaki spirit wearing a mask; none of it is played for gore, and all of it is played for *almost*.

Their doctrine is simple, sincere at the bottom, and terrible at the top. An unfolded world, in their teaching, is an unfinished one — raw, loose at the edges, suffering from distance the way an unbound wound suffers from air — and finishing is mercy. Their home, of which they speak the way monks speak of a garden nobody may visit, is the Finished World: folded long ago to its last crease, complete, at rest. What actually lives at the heart of a finished world is discussed in §5, and the gap between the scripture and the fact is the gap between the order's two floors.

The rank and file believe the mercy. They keep the roads, ride down the breach-monsters, administer the rations with scrupulous fairness, and regard humanity with the patient fondness of a gardener for an unpruned tree. The same patrol that saves a village on Tuesday will fold a protest square shut on Thursday, and it will do both with identical courtesy and identical certainty, a hero twice in its own reckoning and an instrument twice in fact. Their scripture tells them that at the heart of the Finished World lives the Rest, and they understand the Rest to be peace. Their leadership knows what the word is for.

What the leadership wants is completion. The jam is, to them, an unbearable thing — a mercy interrupted, a delivery a lifetime overdue — and everything they do points at the one instrument that can move the great fold. With the Armature they could finish in an afternoon. Without it they can pinch wrinkles and wait, and they have been waiting, with a patience humans should find more frightening than anger, for generations. Beneath the doctrine sits the argument no Surveyor will ever say aloud, which is this: if this world is never finished, then the finishing of their own world was not mercy, and the Rest is not rest, and everything they are and everything they serve is a crime with a liturgy. They would rather complete a second world than reopen the question of the first.

Their numbers are fixed, which explains nearly everything about their governing style. Every Surveyor abroad came through before the jam, and no more can follow until the fold moves, so they spend themselves carefully, prefer human proxies for human work, and have made an entire theory of rule out of never needing to be feared. It is why the Registry is staffed with human clerks, why the rations are genuinely fair, and why the only laws enforced without gentleness are the ones that touch the creases.

They keep the veiled figure on the high seat for three mutually reinforcing reasons. The Foldwright's name carries absolute authority, because nobody would kneel to a ministry of paper men while everybody kneels to the man who folded the world. The figure absorbs every cruelty, so that each fence, each sealed road, and each spreading gray mile belongs to the veil rather than to the courteous beings administering it. And it gives the world's anger somewhere safe to go, which is the function of Green Fields.

The leadership justifies itself with arguments, two of which are true. Unfolding would loose everything in the zones at once onto a world that has forgotten how to fight, and the heroes will have to answer that honestly. And the Surveyors do hold back the breaches; without them, people would die this week. The third argument is the doctrine itself, and it is the one the campaign is finally about, because it will be made to the party's faces in Act Three by a being that believes it: *your world was paying a tax in grief and distance that we abolished; we have governed you more kindly than you ever governed yourselves; and everything ends, little ones — the only choice any world gets is whether it ends in disorder or is folded away finished, complete, and at rest.*

### Green Fields

Green Fields is the rebellion the world is permitted, and it is the occupation's finest work.

Its platform is not that the Fold was a crime, and it is not even that the Surveyors are enemies. That is the crucial thing about it, and it is what makes the whole arrangement stable. Green Fields is grateful — for the safety, for the sunsalt, for the monsters dead in the wheat fields — and it says so in its first breath. What it cannot accept, and says in its second, is that gratitude is not citizenship. Every road, every ration, every law of the world sits with a veiled figure nobody may petition and a ministry nobody elected, and a guest who governs is not a guest. Let human hands hold human things. It is a decent, moderate, deeply reasonable position, and the people who hold it are sincere.

Its most beloved arm is the one that goes in after the taken. When a breach closes over somebody's daughter, the ministry offers courteous regret and a sealed crease, and Green Fields offers a search party. Expeditions into the creases are forbidden by the only laws the Surveyors enforce coldly — and Green Fields' expeditions are invariably, quietly permitted, provided a Registry clerk accompanies them to keep the record. Nobody in the movement has ever found this odd, and nobody has asked why the one unforgivable law bends for them alone, because nobody in the movement knows that the law is their employer.

The organization's leadership is Surveyor. Not allied with the invaders, not infiltrated by them; a handful of them, folded into human shapes, wearing the movement the way their chief wears a title. The disguise is craft rather than sorcery — hours of work and a steady burn of sunsalt — and it is imperfect: somewhere on every folded-in Surveyor there is a crease that does not line up, at a collar, at a wrist, in the shadow of a hairline. The world is full of things that do not quite line up, and nobody looks twice, which is what disguised Surveyors and portals have in common. They built the movement because they needed the one thing they cannot do themselves, which is send searchers into the Snarl after the man with the machine, and every rescue expedition is also a cast of the dragnet. The rank and file have never heard of the Armature. They are looking for the lost. Their masters are looking for what one particular lost man is carrying, and they will know it has surfaced on the day some search party comes back talking about a machine.

The cruelty in it goes further than the deception, and it should be played rather than merely noted. A movement with no martyrs is not believable, so the leadership lets its own sincere members be arrested and folded away by the ministry it serves, and then it grieves them in public and recruits on the strength of the grief. Both halves of that transaction are staffed by honest people. Nobody in the square knows that the raid and the rally were ordered by the same hand.

Green Fields also competes for the haunted, and it gets to them first. This is the quietest cruelty in the campaign. A woman who lays four places for three children is told that the creases take people — everyone knows they do — that her missing one is surely among the taken, and that the movement that searches is her family now. It is a lie of omission and it is close enough to the shape of her wound to fit: the creases did take her fourth child, in the Folding itself, which is the one taking Green Fields will never name, because the Folding is the thing its owners exist to finish. She will spend years searching the wrong creases for the right person, on the payroll of the people who put her child there, and she will be brave about it.

### What Is True of Both

The ministry and Green Fields are one apparatus wearing two coats, and only the Surveyor leadership knows it. The occupation invented a throne to absorb the world's anger and a rebellion to organize its hope, and it owns them both.

The arrangement does three jobs at once, which is why it is such an efficient machine. It searches the Snarl. It absorbs every angry and every grieving person in the world and marches them in a circle. And it owns the alarm, which is the subtlest of the three: the establishment does not suppress fear about the shaking world, it broadcasts that fear itself, through the mouths of people who mean it. Secrets leak, but a publicly acknowledged and permanently parked worry does not. Anyone who notices the quakes worsening already has somewhere to put the feeling, which is that Green Fields is on it.

The corruption is entirely at the top of both, which is what gives this campaign sympathetic enemies, plausible future allies, and a reveal that lands as upsetting rather than clever. The movement was honest, all the way up to the necks of the people wearing it.

### Red Flag

Red Flag is the only real thing in the world, and it is small, and it is growing.

It began where a lie of that size always begins to come apart, which is with somebody who could count. Hettie Sarn ran the Cinder Draw for thirty-one years and personally scraped more sunsalt out of the world than any living person. She is not a mystic and she does not make speeches. She keeps a ledger — the gray ledger, drawn year by year, showing the exact rate at which the dead land has advanced across ground she used to farm — and her method of recruitment is to open it, turn it around, and wait. She is the world's foremost authority on the damage because she did the damage, which means she cannot be dismissed as an idealist, cannot be bought, and is the one person in the world who would most like to be proven wrong.

What the ledger showed her is the thread the whole thing unravels from. The gray is not spreading outward from the creases, as everyone has been told for a lifetime. It is spreading outward from the workings. The blight has a fence around it, and a payroll, and a schedule. And once a person has seen that the establishment is manufacturing the disaster it blames on the veil, it takes very little to see that it is manufacturing the opposition too.

So Red Flag knows what nobody else outside the invaders' own councils knows: that the rebellion answers, somewhere above its own recruiters' heads, to the ministry it claims to petition; that the sunsalt everyone calls a gift is the world being amputated and sold back to itself by the lampful; and that the creases have doors in them. What it does not yet know — and what the party will prove — is that the hands on Green Fields' tiller are not human at all. Their conclusion follows from what they do know, and it is not moderate. The Fold is not a home that needs better management. It is a wound that has been kept open for a lifetime because keeping it open is profitable, and the seams exist only because the world is folded, so there is exactly one way to stop the burning forever, which is to unfold the world and let it be what it was. Open every door. Set it right. Give it back.

They live by that in the plainest way available. An Unlit house — their own word for themselves before Red Flag was ever painted on a wall — burns no sunsalt, so at night its window is the dark one on the street. In its place they keep a tallow candle: a real, honest, ordinary flame of the sort the world has almost forgotten how to make. It is not a signal so much as an argument. Everyone can see the dark window, nobody has worked out what it means, and the invaders, who cannot imagine an enemy that refuses the gift rather than demanding more of it, have never thought to look for one.

Their rank and file are the haunted, which is the difference between them and Green Fields stated in one line. Green Fields tells the woman with the empty chair that her missing one was taken by a breach and might yet be found by searching. Red Flag tells her the truth, which is worse and which she has been waiting her whole life to hear: nothing did this to you by accident, the world was folded with your child inside it, and the only way you will ever see her again is if we open every door there is. Red Flag does not need to persuade the haunted. It needs to survive long enough to reach them, because Green Fields is reaching them first.

Their people are also, in significant part, Green Fields defectors — which is why Green Fields loses cells it cannot account for, and why its rank and file mourn friends who are three hundred miles away in a dark house with a candle in the window, unable to send word without hanging everyone they left behind. The leadership is content to let them be mourned as martyrs, because a martyr is worth more than a member.

What Red Flag does not know is the whole of their tragedy. They do not know that opening every door at once looses everything in the zones onto a world that has forgotten how to fight any of it. They do not know that the man they blame the age for was the only person who ever tried to stop it, or that their own program — unfold, and give it back — is his. And they do not know about the Devouring, because no human does. They are right about the crime, right about the criminals, right about the cure, and catastrophically wrong about the method — and they will learn all of that from the heroes, in the field, one door at a time, which is what turns them from a slogan into a real faction over twenty sessions.

Their arc is therefore the party's arc, and it should be run as one. Open every door → *these zones are full of things and we are about to hand them a world* → find a way to unfold that does not end in a massacre → **the machine.** When they learn the Armature exists, everything else stops mattering to them, and Red Flag becomes the third hand reaching for it.

Why they remain dangerous, and why the finale needs them: Red Flag is the party's ally, honestly and warmly, for two acts. Then the Devouring turns up at the center of everything, and Red Flag does not flinch, because they have been right about every single thing for their entire existence and it has cost them everybody they love. *We have come this far. We are not stopping at the last door because there is something behind it.* Their zeal is the same shape as the Foldwright's love for his family, and it points the same way, and in Act Three the heroes may find that the people who trusted them first are the ones who have to be talked down.

## 4. The Cast

### The Foldwright — the man

The world believes the Foldwright rules it, remade by his own working into something beyond man, veiled on a high seat no petition reaches. The truth is that the title has been worn by his business partner for generations, and the man himself is a fugitive inside his own fold.

He was human, and he is the reason everything happened. He did not fold the world for power over it; he folded it for plenty — an end to want and distance, bought with a substance his partners showed him and a design he did not examine closely enough, because the answer was too beautiful to interrogate. The working swallowed cities, and his own wife and children with them, and midway through it he read the pattern at last and understood that he was folding his world into an envelope with a recipient. He halted the working at the cost of his measure, jammed the design, pinned the half-arrived thing at the center, and fled into the Snarl with the Armature when his partners came to collect. Every grief in the world descends from his signature, and he knows it, which is a heavier thing to carry than the machine.

He is also, in the only sense that matters, the sole human mourner of everything the Folding took. The working stripped the memory of the lost places out of the world and not out of the hands that held the fabric — his, and theirs. The Surveyors remember the cities perfectly well and lost nothing they loved, because nothing they loved was here. He grieves streets nobody else has ever heard of, and a wife and children whose existence no living person will concede, and whatever else is true of the man, that is his condition, and it explains more about him than any of his choices do.

He is not a prisoner. He is a fugitive. Kill him and the jam slips, so until they hold the Armature his enemies dare not touch him even if they could reach him, and they cannot reach him. He has spent every day of a long lifetime working the machine in the dark, holding a half-folded world at the crease, while the world curses the name his enemies wear. The foldquakes are his hands shaking.

He needs the heroes, four nobodies, despite having been the most powerful wizard who ever lived, and for reasons that are worth keeping straight. He is hollowed, and is no longer the man the age is named for. He cannot leave the Snarl, because stepping out would cost him the Armature and end everything, and although he can move within the folds and slip briefly through weak places, he cannot open a portal from the inside — and in any case he cannot spare the strength to try, since every ounce he has is holding the jam. He cannot walk in the world, because the world believes the Foldwright is nine feet tall and veiled, and a shabby old man claiming the name would be laughed at in the square and filed by evening. And the Compass Spirit, which he made himself, will not serve him. It fled its maker and slept a lifetime rather than answer him, and it woke for these heroes and chose them, which is the one thing he can neither command nor purchase. He can only follow.

He is both help and hindrance, and the contradiction is honest rather than a trick. He wants the portals opened, because his family is inside and every opened portal is somewhere he can finally search. He dreads the last of them, because the thing at the center is one door from daylight. He wants both things sincerely, and this is why the party will be unable to read him for two acts. He may also, in the end, still choose his family over the world — he chose the world over everything once, at the halt, and a man who has paid that price once does not obviously pay it twice. Talking him down in Act Three is a scene worth playing, but it is not the finale.

He should be run briefly and then removed. He appears in the creases beyond a portal and never at the seams where the party harvests. He warns in complete sentences that the party will misread — *"You don't know what's on the other side"* will be heard as a warning about monsters — and he never trails off, because an unfinished sentence is a signpost and a complete misleading one is a disguise. He never voices his real fear, because his fear is the reveal.

He could, of course, simply tell them who he is. They would not believe him, because the Foldwright sits veiled on a throne a thousand miles away — and if they did believe him, it would be worse, because then they would be looking at the man who signed for all of it.

### What He Lost, and Where It Is

Three things went into the Snarl with the world: his family, meaning his wife and his children; his apprentices, the only other humans who ever understood a line of the craft, folded away in the working they helped him raise; and his measure, the piece of himself he spent jamming the design, whose absence left him too weak to undo what he had done.

These are scattered through the banished zones, one to a cell — a child in this one, an apprentice in that one, a shard of his measure deeper in. The party's journey inward is therefore also the reassembly of the Foldwright, and by the finale the man the whole world curses will be strong enough to matter, because the heroes will have rebuilt him without understanding that they were doing it.

A shard of his measure needs to be a thing a child can pick up, and the word gives us the image. To take a man's measure is to weigh him, so what he spent is weight, and what is left of him is light — a hollow man, in the plainest sense the word has. A shard is therefore a small dull object, plain enough to be mistaken for a paperweight, which is impossibly heavy for its size and which nothing in the world will make lighter. It does nothing. It cannot be spent, sold, or used, and it is worth carrying anyway, and every one the party finds makes their packs harder to lift. The last time they see one it is in his hands, and the man they have been following stands differently.

This structure earns its place three times over. It gives every zone an emotional payload beyond the mechanical business of unlocking a crease. It answers the otherwise fatal question of how a hollowed fugitive could possibly be useful in Act Three. And it arms the real conflict of the endgame, which is that his priorities and the world's priorities are not the same, and the heroes have spent twenty sessions serving both without being told.

It carries a serious risk, which is recorded here rather than hidden. If every cell holds only *his* losses, then the campaign's emotional spine belongs to a non-player character and the table spends twenty sessions fetching an old man's family. The correction is in §8, and it is the highest priority open question in this document: each cell must hold a piece of the Foldwright **and** a piece of a player's story.

### The First Surveyor — the false Foldwright

The being on the high seat has worn a dead man's name for so long that the name may fit better than the face it was taken from, and the face is the point: nobody has ever seen it, because none may look upon the face that folded the world, and the veil goes from crown to floor.

The First Surveyor is the one who made the bargain. It measured the wizard the way its kind measures everything, offered him exactly the future he already wanted, and watched him sign. It has governed the half-folded world in his name ever since, with courtesy, restraint, and a patience that should read at the table as the most frightening thing in the campaign — because anger can be waited out, and this thing *is* the waiting. It does not hate humanity any more than a bookbinder hates a manuscript. It intends to finish us, in both senses at once, and it uses the word *finish* with complete sincerity in each.

It is the only being in the world that holds every fact this document holds. It knows the throne is a costume, because it is wearing it. It knows what the Rest is, because its own world was fed to the Rest, and it has built its life on never reopening that question. And it knows the Foldwright personally — his gifts, his grief, his signature — which is why the endgame's argument belongs to the two of them, with the heroes standing where the world's decision actually gets made. The Devouring is an appetite, and an appetite cannot argue; the First Surveyor is the campaign's arguing villain, and its case, delivered gently in Act Three, is the doctrine at full strength: everything ends, and the only mercy any world is offered is to end folded, finished, and at rest, rather than raw and screaming in the dark. It believes this. Whether anything could unconvince it — and what it would cost the being to be unconvinced — is an open question in §8, and the answer may be the difference between a finale the table wins and a finale the table earns.

Two notes on running it. It should be met early and often in its administrative aspect — a tall courteous presence at a ribbon-cutting, a voice thanking a village for its patience — so that Act Three is a betrayal by somebody rather than a debut by nobody. And its human disguise, if it ever wears one, must be flawless except for one crease that does not line up, because that is the rule for all of them and the party will have learned to look.

### The Compass Spirit

The Compass Spirit is a shy creature, a fragment of the world's living connective tissue, the thing that once held distant places in relation to one another. It curls up to about the size of a sleeping cat, with rather less of a cat's confidence and rather more of its opinions, and it gives off roughly the light of a firefly, which is to say enough to find it by and not enough to read by. Fed, it unfurls into a glowing compass rose thrown into the air or across the ground, indicating the way to the next portal.

It is the only thing on the human side of anything that can navigate the Snarl, because it is the only thing that still remembers how the places used to touch. Without it the Fold is an unmappable labyrinth, and this is what allows four nobodies to accomplish what armies could not.

It does a second thing, and the second thing is the campaign's evidence. A memory is a relation between a person and a place, and relation is precisely the substance this creature is made of, so what the Folding creased shut the Compass Spirit can briefly open. Fed at a seam and set down where a lost thing used to be, it gives a memory back — a street, a harbor, a face, an afternoon — to whoever is standing there. The heroes will therefore carry around with them a handful of memories that no other living person possesses, which is the proof Red Flag needs most and which is worth exactly nothing to anyone who was not standing there when it happened. Learning that a true thing cannot be handed to somebody is one of the harder lessons this campaign has to teach, and it should be taught by letting them try.

It drinks sunsalt, which is not its own substance but the only fuel left in the world, and so every direction and every memory costs a gray handprint left behind. Red Flag has counted handprints of exactly that kind, in a ledger, for thirty years.

Its directions are riddles rather than bearings — *the tower that drinks the sun*, or *where two rivers forget each other* — which is not an affectation. In a place with no straight lines a bearing would be useless; the spirit can only describe what a place is like, never which way it lies.

The Foldwright made it, and it will not serve him.

### Wenna Ash — the recruiter

Wenna Ash lays four places at her table and has three children. She has done it for as long as she can remember, she cannot explain it, and for most of her life the people around her have been gentle with her in the way that people are gentle with the unwell.

Green Fields found her, and gave her the only explanation anybody has ever offered: that the creases take people — everyone knows the creases take people — that her missing one is surely among the taken, and that the movement that goes in after the taken is where a woman like her belongs. She has believed that for six years, and searched on the strength of it, and recruited others like herself. It is not true. It is not quite a lie either, and it is close enough to the shape of her wound to fit, which is why it has held. The creases did take her fourth child — in the Folding itself, before the bad time, before the breaches, in the one taking her employers will never name.

She is a true believer, and she is nowhere near the top of anything. She has never met the people who give the orders, she has never heard of the Armature, and she could not tell the heroes what her expeditions are really for because she does not know. Everything she says to them she means. When the betrayal lands it will not land on her; it will land through her, and she will be as ruined by it as they are — and she should be on the page when it happens, which is why she must be a person to them long before then.

The cruelest fact in her file is that she is one of the haunted, which means she belongs to Red Flag and always did. The people who took her fourth child are the people she searches for, and she is brave for them, and she recruits others like herself. Getting her out is a payoff worth holding until late.

One caution on running her. Wenna does not know what was taken, only that something is missing; she has a fourth place setting and no theory, and she must never be permitted to explain the memory-theft, because she has never heard of it. Green Fields does not preach that the Folding stole anything. That is Red Flag's line, and she has not met them yet.

### Tobin Rell — the clerk who was sent to watch them

The ministry sends a man with the party, and the man is not a spy in any sense a nine-year-old would recognize. Tobin Rell is a junior clerk of the Registry — the human-staffed office that records what crosses a road, what comes out of a Draw, and who was standing nearby, because the Surveyors prefer human hands for human paperwork and like everything written down. He is attached to the party's expedition under the one law that always bends: no one enters the creases without a clerk to keep the record. He was chosen because he is cheap, because he is expendable, and above all because he can write a legible report. He has never been in a fight. He is not brave, he is not deft, and he will spend the first half of the campaign being protected by children.

What he is, is a very good clerk. He writes down what he sees, accurately, every night, because that is what he was told to do and he has never in his life done a job badly. That habit — and not courage, and not conscience — is what destroys the people who hired him, because a man who records the truth faithfully for a year ends up holding a year of the truth.

He is not Red Flag when the campaign begins. He is a man who has begun to doubt, who has been quietly approached once, and who has not said yes. His doubt has a name, and the name is a friend of his who went into the folds with a Green Fields expedition and did not come back, and whom Tobin has been dutifully mourning for two years. The friend is not dead. He walked, and he is alive, in a dark house, three hundred miles away, with a candle in the window.

He is also not Wenna's man, and the party should be allowed to work that out. Wenna did not ask for him and does not question him, because a movement whose expeditions have always carried clerks does not notice being watched. He was assigned from above her by an office she has never dealt with, and he reports to it and not to her. The first time the heroes see Tobin file something Wenna has not been told about is the first loose thread in this campaign, and it is available in the first three sessions.

The party's evidence is what pushes Tobin over, and the moment that finishes him is not an argument, it is being taken to see his friend. That scene does three jobs at once and is the reason he exists: it proves the rebellion is on a leash, it hands the heroes the only real faction in the world, and it does both by way of a man finding out that the person he has grieved for two years is alive and could not write. Red Flag does not recruit the heroes. It inherits them, through him.

Afterward he takes his year of notes and goes, because Red Flag needs what is in them and because he is finally, for the first time in his life, doing a job for somebody he chose. The party loses their most useless companion and gains their only honest witness, and if it has been played well they will miss him badly.

Two notes on running him. He should be a genuine burden in a fight for a long time, because a party that has to keep an adult alive will grow attached to him whether they mean to or not, and because the inversion is funny and the children will enjoy it. And he must not be load-bearing: Red Flag exists without him, and if he dies in a bad fight in session six, Hettie Sarn finds the party some other way.

### Still to Cast

The campaign still needs the Foldwright's wife and children, who are the whole of what he is walking toward; it needs a name and a self for the First Surveyor beyond its office; and it has acquired one new casting question worth weighing, which is whether any rank-and-file Surveyor ever chooses the unfinished world. See §8.

## 5. The Threat

### Two Tiers, and Never Confuse Them

The campaign has two kinds of monster and the whole architecture depends on keeping them apart.

**Tier two** is everything in the banished zones: trolls, wyrms, wild magic, things with teeth. Ordinary, familiar, satisfying to fight, and the reason unfolding carelessly is a massacre rather than a liberation. These are the campaign's encounter engine, because the portals lead into the zones and the zones are the dungeons. The Surveyors stand beside tier two in the party's early experience — enemies met with force — but they are not monsters and do not die, and §7's rules keep the distinction honest.

**Tier one** is the Devouring, and there is only one of it. It is not a large tier-two monster and it is not the king of them. It is a different category of thing, it is the entire reason the world was folded and the entire reason the folding was halted, and it must never be hinted at as one of the others.

The reason to name this split plainly is that the party will spend two acts solving a tier-two problem — *how do we open the doors without loosing all of that at once* — and the answer they arrive at, which is the machine, is a genuinely good answer to the wrong question. The machine can stage an unfolding. It cannot make the thing at the center be gone, and the last door is a decision the machine cannot take for anyone.

### The Banished-Zone Monsters

Tier two is also the decoy that protects the reveal, and this is the most important structural fact in the campaign. Everyone knows the Fold walls off monsters and everyone knows unfolding lets them out, so when a player asks what happens if the world comes apart, the answer is available, satisfying, and entirely true: all of that gets out. The question is asked, answered, and closed, and nobody digs any further.

The Devouring hides behind a satisfying answer rather than behind a blank space.

### The Devouring

The Devouring sits at the center of the Snarl, pinned in the jam, half arrived, and it is still eating.

It is the resident of the Finished World, and the reason finished worlds are finished. The Surveyors' scripture calls it the Rest and understands it as peace; their leadership's sealed ledgers call it the Last Fold and understand it exactly. A world folded to its final crease is not a monument but a delivery, and everything of this thing that had come through to receive ours was caught when the Foldwright jammed the design — locked out of its arrival, pinned at the one point no halt could close, eating outward ever since, because eating makes room and room is how it finishes arriving.

No human alive believes it exists, because no human has ever been told: not the public, not Green Fields, not Red Flag, not the Registry's clerks. Among the Surveyors the belief splits by floor, and the split is generational doctrine rather than conspiracy — the rank and file know the Rest as a word in their liturgy, the way a child knows the name of a distant grandmother, and would be genuinely bewildered to hear it called a threat. Only the leadership knows the word's referent is real, present, and pinned at the center of this world, and the leadership calls that fact not a danger but a delivery overdue.

Its face and its fight remain undecided, deliberately, but one constraint on that decision is already fixed. It must end up describable and fightable. This table will have spent twenty sessions swinging swords, and the finale cannot be an abstraction or a conversation.

## 6. The Arc

### The Opening — the Town Above

The heroes are in a city, for four reasons of their own that are not yet written, and they are crossing a square.

A man is shouting in it. He has a box to stand on and a voice that has been used too hard, and what he is shouting is not treason, or not quite. He is saying that the Fold is the best thing that ever happened to anybody, that his own grandmother walks to market through country that used to eat travellers, and that not one word of that explains why no human being sits at any table where anything is decided. Gratitude is not citizenship. Ask their leave to visit your sister. Ask their leave to sell your grain. Thank them for the fences, and ask whose world this is, exactly, and watch the courteous faces fold into courteous smiles and answer nothing. Half the square agrees with him and keeps walking, because he says it every week; the other half has somewhere to be; and there are two Surveyors at the edge of it, tall and mild and very slightly wrong against the light, not intervening, writing down faces.

Above him, as above every city, hangs the town above: the inverted underside of some other country, close enough here to make out roofs, and a road, and a clock tower with hands on it that the children of this city have watched all their lives and have never once been able to reach.

Then the ground moves.

It is a foldquake, and it is a bad one, and what it does is shake the tower loose from the sky. A city's worth of masonry comes down out of the ceiling of the world, end over end, taking three seconds to arrive, and the square becomes the worst thing that has ever happened in it. That is the first thing the heroes see. The second thing is that something came down with the tower — that the crease opened as it tore, and things with teeth came through the gap, and they have landed in a crowded square and are hungry.

The heroes fight, which is the point, and the fight is inside ten minutes. The Surveyors fight too, at the far end of the square, with a terrible unfolding efficiency — and when it is over they seal the gap, bow to the survivors, and resume writing, which the party should notice without being asked to.

Afterward, in the dust, with the clock face lying broken in the road and its hands still moving, a woman comes up to them who has been in the square the whole time and who did not run. Her name is Wenna Ash, she has been with Green Fields for six years, and she has just watched four strangers do in ninety seconds what her entire movement prays for, which is stand between the gap and somebody's grandmother. She does not give them a speech. She tells them that people went into that gap before it closed — she saw a boy pulled through, and nobody will say his name tomorrow because saying it is called *alarmism* — that the shaking is worse every year and the fences go up and never come down; that her movement is the only one that goes in after the taken, and that the ministry permits it and records it and never, ever helps; and that what they need, and have needed for years, is somebody who can go in and come back out. Every word she believes. Most of it is true. And the two Surveyors at the edge of the square have written down four new faces and will file them by evening, which is how the heroes acquire a clerk.

Why each of the four heroes happens to be in that city on that morning is not yet written, because that is the seam where the players' own material goes, and it is being held open until we have it. See §8.

### How the Mission Escalates

The party's task never changes and its meaning changes six times, which is what makes it playable for twenty sessions by a nine-year-old. The operational verb is always the same — *find the next door, go through it, and bring back what is on the other side* — and it acquires a new significance at each rung.

1. **Go in and bring back the taken.** People are lost in the creases and nobody else will go. This is the whole of the brief Green Fields gives them, and Wenna means it.
2. **The portals, and the Unfold Rule.** Inside, the party discovers that the regions are locked, that each has a door, and that no crease can be pulled open while the others are still set. The mission acquires a shape: all of them, or none of them.
3. **The fold is jammed, and something is holding it.** Deeper in it becomes plain that the world is straining against an instrument, that the holding is being done by a made thing rather than a will, and that a made thing can be taken. Nobody told them this. The mission acquires an object.
4. **The machine has a keeper, and the keeper is a man.** Not a Surveyor, not a veil on a throne — a shabby, hollow, human man, in the dark ahead of them, and the ground stops shaking for as long as they are near him. The mission acquires a face — and when the party sends word of the machine back to the people who hired them, the answer comes far too quickly, and asks about nothing else, and does not ask about the taken at all.
5. **Act Two.** The throne is a costume. The keeper is the Foldwright, the folding was a bargain he refused to finish paying, and the people who sent them are the sellers, still waiting on delivery.
6. **Act Three.** There is something at the center, the fold was an envelope addressed to it, and the last door is a decision rather than a dungeon.

Two things follow from this and both are load-bearing. The machine is *discovered* rather than briefed, so the Armature must not be named in Act One. And the heroes have time to become people who could plausibly take it, which means the campaign has a structural stake in leveling.

### Why Unfolding Is Not Madness

The jam cannot hold, because something is eating it, and nothing available to anyone will stop that. The choice facing the heroes is therefore not whether the world comes apart but how, and in whose direction.

If they do nothing, one of two things happens. Either the jam slips and the half-fold springs open on its own — every banished zone bursting at once into a world with no warning, no walls, and no living memory of how to fight what comes out — or the Surveyors finally take the Armature, and the fold closes, and the letter is delivered.

If the heroes unfold deliberately, they go through the portals one at a time, and in each zone they get the lost people out, learn the land and what lives in it, recover the old ways of fighting the things there, and mark the door. When the world finally comes apart, it comes apart into a world that knows what is coming.

Stated for the table, and it should be said aloud: the wall is going to fall, and we can be standing there ready or asleep in bed.

This is the answer to the campaign's hardest question, and it needs to be in front of the players by early Act Two and never allowed to go vague. It is also what every region is *for*. The party is not ticking boxes; each zone they open is a zone that will not be a massacre.

It is not, however, the end of the argument, and it should not be allowed to become one. The counter is held by ordinary people and by the sincere whole of Green Fields, and it is not stupid: the Fold works, it has worked for a lifetime, children play at the treeline because of it, and four strangers with a magic firefly propose to take that away on the strength of something they saw behind a door nobody else can open. Every word of that is fair. The heroes are asking a world to give up the only safety it has ever known, on their word, and the world does not owe them the benefit of the doubt.

### The Three Acts

**Act One.** *The rebellion is on a leash, and the hands on the leash are not human.* Breaches and foldquakes worsen, this season, where the heroes live. The gray turns out to be the Draws rather than a blight. Their own clerk turns out to be filing reports their recruiter never sees. The movement they joined loses none of its sincerity and all of its innocence — and the act's last image should be the one the campaign has been saving: a Green Fields officer, cornered or careless, coming undone along a crease that never quite lined up, folding apart into a drift of panes where a person stood. The heroes were not fighting the occupation. They were bird-dogging for it.

The turn of Act One is Tobin's friend, alive in a dark house, and everything that follows from a man being mourned by the organization that knows perfectly well where he is. It delivers the betrayal and hands the party Red Flag in the same scene. The second half of the turn is Red Flag's own claim, which is larger and which the heroes will spend the act testing: the Fold did not merely cost something, it robbed the world of the memory of what it cost, and the answer is not better management of the Fold but no Fold at all.

**Act Two.** *The throne is a costume, and the tyrant is the world's only defense.* The veiled almighty has never been the man; the man is a hollowed human fugitive holding the jam in the dark, and every decree of the age was issued by the beings who sell the world its lamps. Here the party learns the bargain — that the Folding was a purchase, that their world was the payment, and that the man they were sent to find is the one who signed and the only one who refused to deliver. The act closes on the question of what, exactly, he refused to deliver it *to*.

**Act Three.** *The Devouring, and the two Foldwrights.* It is real, it is at the center, it has been eating the entire time, and the fold was always an envelope. The First Surveyor makes the case for finishing — gently, sincerely, to their faces — and it is the best argument any villain in this campaign will ever make. Red Flag, who have been right about everything at the cost of everyone, must be talked out of opening the last door blind. And the Foldwright — restored, with his family a single door away — may still choose them over the world, as he nearly did once. The heroes hold the machine, the argument, and the decision.

### The Act One Clock

The breaches and the foldquakes are worsening this season, in the place where the heroes live. The pressure is visible, urgent, fightable, and blamed by everyone on the wrong name. The world-scale truth is what the heroes learn; the monster in the wheat field is what makes them go.

## 7. Running It

### The Load-Bearing Rules

Several of the rules below exist to keep the later revelations from leaking, and breaking one of them will quietly break something downstream. Rules one and two exist for a different reason — they are what this campaign is *for*, at a table with a nine- and a ten-year-old — and they outrank everything else here.

1. **No human being is ever a combat encounter.** Humans in this campaign argue, obstruct, inform, grieve, and defect; they are never fought. The things the party fights are Surveyors, zone monsters, and eventually the Devouring. If a scene is drifting toward swords drawn on a person, restage the scene.
2. **Surveyors come undone; they do not die.** Struck down, a Surveyor folds apart into a drift of panes and is gone, and some weeks later it is somewhere else, itself again, and it remembers. There are no Surveyor corpses, no gore, and no guilt — and there are recurring enemies, because the one the party bested in the spring can bow to them, courteously, in the fall.
3. The banished zones hold ordinary monsters. The Devouring is categorically different and is never hinted at as one of them.
4. Every seed planted for the Devouring must have a satisfying wrong answer available. Never leave a blank, because a gap is a signpost. If the Compass Spirit is afraid, it should be afraid in a monster's lair, so that the party concludes it fears the monster.
5. No human alive believes the Devouring exists. The Surveyor rank and file know it only as the Rest, a word of scripture meaning peace; only their leadership knows the word's referent is real, present, and pinned at the center. Red Flag does not know, and learns it last.
6. The world believes the Folding was clean, and believes the figure on the throne is the Foldwright, remade by his own working. Both beliefs are corroborated daily — the first by a sky full of neat creases, the second by a veil nobody may lift — and only the Surveyor leadership knows both are false.
7. The Folding took the memory of what it took, and it took it from the world and not from the hands that held the fabric — the Foldwright's, and the Surveyors'. He is therefore the only human who mourns, the invaders remember everything and lost nothing, and the haunted are the people the theft botched. Nobody explains the mechanism, at the table or in the fiction, because no human understands it. State the fact and show the evidence.
8. No human knows what the Fold really is. The folded-sheet diagram is for us; the Surveyors know precisely what they are standing in, and never say. The Snarl is a revelation, and it must land from the inside by being walked through; no character may describe it to the party before the party has stood in it.
9. The fold is jammed at its midpoint and strains both ways. Completion has a recipient. The deep design answers only to the Armature — wrinkles are surface pinches, and nobody, human or Surveyor, can fold the great fold by hand.
10. The Armature is not named in Act One, and neither Green Fields' rank and file nor Red Flag has ever heard of it. Wenna's brief is to go in and bring back the taken; the machine is a discovery the party earns at the third rung of §6, and its power as a goal depends entirely on their having found it themselves. Only the Surveyor leadership knows it exists, which is why their sudden interest when the party reports it is the loudest clue in Act Two.
11. The Foldwright never explains himself until Act Three, and the First Surveyor is never unveiled until Act Two.
12. Sunsalt is the Finished World's substance. Refined, it folds — it fuels every wrinkle and every disguise. Raw, it unfolds — and it is the only fuel the great Unfold can burn. Drawing it kills the land it is drawn from, permanently, which is what makes the finale a genuine sacrifice.
13. Wrinkles cost sunsalt, heavily.
14. The Armature is the standoff. The invaders cannot finish without it, cannot find the man who holds it, and dare not shake his grip; he cannot come out, and cannot unfold alone, because he is hollow. Nobody has moved in a lifetime. The heroes are what breaks the deadlock — and if they find him, they lead the hunters to him.
15. Green Fields never attacks the Fold and never names the Surveyors as enemies. Its platform is gratitude plus representation — human hands on human things — and the moment it radicalizes past that, it collides with Red Flag, the two rebellions stop being distinguishable, and the reason the occupation tolerates one and hunts the other evaporates.
16. Every faction has two floors. The Surveyor leadership knows everything; its rank and file believe the mercy. Green Fields' leadership is that same leadership in folded human shapes; its rank and file are sincere. Red Flag's leadership knows the crime and not the cost. Never let a faction speak with one voice.

### The Words We Use at the Table

The glossary at the end of this document runs past two dozen entries, and a nine-year-old will not carry two dozen proper nouns. What will break this campaign at the table is not the conspiracy, which is simple enough to state in four sentences, but the vocabulary, which is not.

The rule is therefore that the bible keeps the real words and the table gets plain ones. Say the real word once, in the moment where it sounds best, and then let it go; the children will settle on their own word for everything, and their word will be better than ours. The Surveyors themselves are the clearest case: the children will have named them by the end of the first session, and their name is the one the campaign should use forever after.

### Why the Propaganda Holds

The official story rests on two claims, and each is half true, which is what makes it durable. The first is that the Folding was a rescue that cost nothing — and the safety is real, the plenty is real, and the cost is unrememberable, so the true half is verified daily and the false half cannot even be stated as a question. The second is that the Foldwright rules — and the age really is his work, his name really is on everything, and the veil on the high seat guarantees that the one witness who could contradict the story is precisely the one nobody may look at.

Nobody is being kept in the dark about the danger, because the danger is advertised, with courteous regret, by the ministry itself. And nobody is being kept in the dark about the losses, because as far as the world is aware there were none — only the taken, whose absence is permitted to be mourned precisely because it can be blamed on the creases and answered with a search party the occupation happens to own.

### The Three Props

The best props available to this campaign are made of paper, and there are three of them.

The first is the folded sheet: a real map, neatly folded, with a seal — a paper clip, a wax dot, a ribbon — on every crease. This is the ministry-issue diagram, and it should be handed to the party in Act One like a pamphlet. Unlocking a region means removing that fold's seal while the map stays folded, so that the children watch the checklist shrink and never once need to ask whether the world has unfolded yet. The last seal coming off is the finale. The Unfold Rule can be demonstrated in the same breath by pulling one crease open while the others are still set, and letting the paper tear.

The second is the Snarl: another map, crumpled into a tight wad and forced into a small box, kept out of sight until the party first goes deep enough to see the truth. It is then placed on the table with a single line — *this is the real one*.

The third is an envelope, and it is held back the longest, because it is Act Three in an object. A letter is folded in order to be sent. When the time comes to say what the fold was for, fold a paper in front of them, slide it into the envelope, and ask who it is addressed to.

There is a fourth prop, and it costs nothing: a plain tallow candle, produced the first time the party spends a night in a dark house.

### Tone and Guardrails

The register is family-friendly with a light edge, leaning family. Spooky villains, real stakes, and gentle dark themes — betrayal, loss, a comfortable lie — are all welcome, as is cheeky humor that winks at the adults. The Surveyors should be run for courteous wrongness rather than horror: the fright of a too-tall figure folding itself under a door is the fright this campaign wants, and it is a fright a nine-year-old enjoys.

There is no permanent character death. Heroes are knocked out, captured, or rescued, and never killed off. Nobody in this world is executed, on any side: the ministry folds people away, and the folded-away can be found. There is no explicit gore — the enemies that fall in battle are monsters, and the enemies that matter come undone into paper and reassemble somewhere else.

There is no long exposition. The children get hooked quickly and the story reveals itself through doing rather than through telling.

And it is not merely a series of battles. Every leg of the journey wants a fight, a puzzle — the Compass riddles carry most of that load — and one moment in which somebody has to decide something uncomfortable.

### Every Faction Is Partly Right

This is worth protecting, because it is the difference between a morally interesting campaign and a series of fights.

The townsfolk love the folded world and are not fools; it genuinely is safer and warmer than what came before, and the people who defend the arrangement are defending their children, not their chains. The rank-and-file Surveyors save real lives at every breach and believe, all the way down, that finishing is mercy. The rank and file of Green Fields want nothing more radical than a human voice at the table, and their movement's search parties are the only ones that ever go in after the taken. The First Surveyor is correct that unfolding loose is a massacre, correct that the world has been governed more gently than it ever governed itself, and wrong about the one word its whole civilization is built on. Red Flag is correct about the gray, correct about the leash, correct that the seams end only when the Fold does — and wrong about the method in a way that would kill thousands, which they will learn from the heroes and not from us. The Foldwright bought the catastrophe with the best intentions anyone ever had, and then paid more than anyone has ever paid to stop it, which means both his enemies and his mourners are owed their opinion of him. And the heroes are right that the Fold must come down, but the cost is real and it will be paid by people with names.

In fact: a human wizard who bought the Folding believing he was buying plenty, halted it at the cost of himself when he read the pattern, and has held the jam in the dark ever since — the age's author, its only human mourner, and its only defense. Real name undecided. |
| **the Compass Spirit** | The companion. It navigates the Snarl, and it gives back memories the Folding took. It will not serve its maker. Name undecided. |
| **the Devouring** | The real threat, and the recipient. The resident of the Finished World, for which finished worlds are folded; the Surveyors' scripture calls it the Rest and their sealed ledgers call it the Last Fold. Everything of it that had come through to receive the world was pinned at the center by the jam, half arrived, and it eats outward — because eating makes room, and room is how it finishes arriving. |
